Informational Power Through Pivotality: How Consensus Can Benefit a Hegemon

Date: 05/24/2026 Domain: social_sciences/political_science Taxonomy: academic/working_paper Filter: Active comments

Overall Feedback

Here are some overall reactions to the document.

Outline

The paper has a real mechanism and is careful about separating what is assessment-free from what is selection-dependent, but the current draft still asks the reader to accept several strong modeling choices that do most of the comparative work. The main concerns are whether the public-voting equilibrium selection is justified tightly enough, whether the majority benchmark is too favorable by construction, and whether the institutional-design framing outruns what the benchmark actually proves.

The paper’s best feature is conceptual discipline. It isolates one channel, informational leverage created by unanimity when the hegemon is pivotal and privately informed, and it is more careful than most formal IR papers about stating that the result is conditional rather than a blanket ranking. The remaining problem is that the assumptions needed to isolate that channel currently narrow the contribution enough that the result still feels benchmark-specific rather than fully persuasive as a general account of consensus and hegemonic power.

The main unanimity result leans too heavily on the weak-vote-passive assessment

Definition 2 and Appendix A.3 do much more than tidy up off-path beliefs. They determine which rejected histories are informative, allow weak-caused failure to collapse to the delay candidate D, and rule out other interpretations of public voting that could change Proposition 2. The justification offered in Section 4, that weak states do not observe H’s type and therefore their unilateral deviations should not signal it, is not enough once votes are public and simultaneous, because a deviant weak vote can still reveal strategic expectations about H’s behavior or intended coordination. This matters because the candidate set P, L, and D, the existence of the delay branch, and the later entry and payoff classification all rest on this assessment-dependent reduction. A stronger revision would either derive this assessment from a more primitive protocol, for example by making weak votes nonpublic or by changing the voting order, or add a robustness section showing which parts of Proposition 2 and Corollary 1 survive under alternative belief disciplines.

The majority benchmark is doing too much substantive work

Section 5 identifies majority with a full-surplus no-H coalition path, with V_W^M = 1/m in equation (1), and then treats departures from that benchmark as peripheral unless Strict No-Cheap-H fails. That is analytically clean, but in the substantive applications the ability to exclude the hegemon while preserving the same unit surplus is exactly what needs defense, not a harmless normalization. In the OPEC illustration especially, excluding Saudi is likely to change the size and enforceability of the coalition surplus, so the contrast between unanimity screening and majority exclusion may partly reflect an imposed efficiency advantage for majority rather than pure informational pivotality. This matters because Proposition 3’s nesting result and much of the institutional classification follow once majority keeps the full pie while unanimity must either transfer to H or delay. The paper would be stronger if Section 5 formally carried through the rho < 1 extension it mentions, or at minimum derived the threshold on rho under which the main comparative statics and the sign of Delta_H still look as claimed.

The institutional ranking remains mostly a possibility result

Proposition 4 says H prefers unanimity when Delta_H(mu) > 0 and majority when Delta_H(mu) < 0, but that is essentially a restatement of equation (26). The hard question is where those regions lie in primitives once candidate selection, entry, and continuation values are combined. Outside the worked example in Section 8 and the restricted grid sweep in Appendix B.4, the paper does not provide sharp sufficient or necessary conditions for unanimity to beat majority on a nontrivial belief set. That leaves the main takeaway closer to a demonstration that the mechanism can occur than to a characterization of when it occurs, which is thinner than the title and introduction suggest. A revision should derive primitive inequalities for the sign of Delta_H within each candidate region, characterize when the both-form set is nonempty, and show how those conditions move with beta, the threshold gap, outside payoffs, and m rather than leaving the substantive burden to Example 1.

The collective-entry stage is too mechanical to carry the institutional-design claims

Section 7 turns formation into an all-or-nothing decision by symmetric weak states with a common per-capita cost chi, and Proposition 3 then delivers F_U(chi) subset F_M(chi) because unanimity either transfers part of the unit surplus to H or delays. That is internally coherent, but it also means the formation result is mostly an accounting identity under the fixed-pie normalization rather than a richer theory of institutional choice. Readers interested in institutional design will want to know whether the main conclusion survives with heterogeneous weak states, partial participation, or entry costs that depend on coalition composition and the rule itself. This matters because the paper repeatedly presents an institutional classification, yet the formation margin that generates the classification is structurally thin. The revision path is either to enrich the entry stage enough that formation is not mechanically tied to average weak payoffs, or to scale back the design claims and present Proposition 3 as a bookkeeping benchmark rather than a substantive institutional theorem.

The benchmark strips out agenda power, but the paper does not show whether the mechanism survives once agenda power returns

Setting pi_H = 0 is a sensible way to isolate the screening channel, and the paper is admirably explicit about doing so in the introduction, Section 2, and Appendix B.3. The problem is that the omitted cases are not side details: in many of the paper’s target applications the hegemon has at least some formal or informal agenda influence, and the author notes that the H-proposer branch is selection-sensitive. As written, the paper therefore proves the mechanism in a deliberately anti-hegemonic benchmark but leaves open whether the headline substantive claim remains informative in the more realistic region where proposal power and pivotality interact. This matters for external relevance because a reader cannot tell whether informational pivotality is a standalone channel or a benchmark artifact that is swamped once pi_H > 0. The fix is not to solve the entire extension exhaustively, but the paper needs more than a paragraph: lower bounds, comparative statics, or simulations for pi_H = 1/N and pi_H > 1/N would go a long way, and absent that the title and abstract should signal much more clearly that the result is a pi_H = 0 benchmark.

The empirical and observable implications remain too verbal

Section 9 gives a thoughtful OPEC interpretation and a short list of observable patterns, but the paper stops just before turning those ideas into disciplined predictions. The discussion says package generosity should vary with uncertainty about the pivotal actor’s threshold and that failed proposals should be informative only when the hegemon’s rejection blocks passage, yet these claims are not formalized as propositions, mapped to observable indicators, or contrasted systematically with agenda-control and complete-information veto stories. Without that bridge, the application reads more as an illustration of vocabulary than as evidence that the model changes how we should read real IO politics. This is a significant omission for a top political science venue, where a formal paper usually needs a clearer path from equilibrium objects to case-facing implications. The revision should add a compact proposition-style section translating P, L, and D into observable meeting outcomes, identify what would distinguish informational pivotality from simpler veto or agenda-power explanations, and show those predictions on the OPEC example or another concrete institutional setting.

The one-dimensional fixed-pie package space may make screening too automatic

In Section 4, y is a relative institutional package that is always feasible and reduces weak surplus one-for-one. That move avoids the old feasibility problems, but it also makes the model very close to a transfer game with a relabeled transfer variable. Once packages are always feasible and linearly costly to weak states, pooling rents under unanimity arise almost mechanically whenever H is pivotal and thresholds differ. The paper needs a stronger argument that this is not just a convenient normalization but a politically meaningful description of quota flexibility, enforcement exceptions, or monitoring burdens. A useful fix would be to show that the main propositions are unchanged under a broader class of weak-cost functions, or to offer a sharper microfoundation for why institutional packages aggregate to a single linear y in the target applications.

No worked institutional phase diagram

Corollary 1 is the paper’s main institutional object, but the paper never fixes an entry cost and actually draws the partition it claims to deliver. Table 4 reports weak-state payoffs at a handful of beliefs, yet readers never see where \mathcal{C}_0(\chi), \mathcal{C}_M(\chi), \mathcal{C}_U^H(\chi), \mathcal{C}_T^H(\chi), and \mathcal{C}_M^H(\chi) lie for any concrete \chi. That leaves the classification oddly abstract, especially because the conclusion presents it as the paper’s clean takeaway. A top-journal theory paper normally shows the phase diagram once it has done the algebra. The fix is straightforward: for Example 1, choose one or two entry costs in the informative range, such as \chi=0.03 and \chi=0.035, and then plot the exact cut lines \chi=V_W^U(\mu) and \chi=1/12, together with the \Delta_H(\mu)=0 crossing at about \mu=0.943. A single heat map over (\mu,\chi) would show at a glance that the headline classification is nonempty and how large the unanimity-favoring region really is.

Second dynamic example is still missing

The main worked example shuts down much of the paper’s extra machinery. It sets o_0=o_1, never selects D, and ends up looking close to the terminal screening logic introduced in Section 3. Appendix B.5 shows one delay point, but one row in a table is not enough to demonstrate what the two-round structure adds to the paper’s central comparison. Readers need one full case in which continuation values change the selected path over a nontrivial belief interval. The cleanest revision is to promote the Appendix B.5 parameterization N=13, \beta=0.9, t_0=o_0=0.10, t_1=o_1=0.50 or a nearby vector, then compute the full P/L/D regions, \Delta_H(\mu), and F_U(\chi) across \mu. If you want cleaner algebra, an alternative is to solve the full two-round N=3 model in closed form and use that as the paper’s dynamic benchmark.

Group-size implications are not computed

All of the paper’s numerical content fixes N=13, even though membership size enters the model directly through m, the majority vote price \beta/m, unanimity-side voter compensation, and the entry margins. That makes it hard to tell whether the result is an OPEC-sized curiosity or a broader statement about voting rules in international organizations. This is not a cosmetic robustness check. Assembly size is one of the first substantive margins readers will ask about. A short simulation would solve the problem: hold the Example 1 primitives fixed and recompute the L/P/D boundaries, the zero of \Delta_H, and the both-form region for N \in \{3,5,7,13,25\} under strict-majority q. Reporting how the unanimity-favoring region moves with N would turn an abstract comparative static into a concrete institutional implication.

Formal nesting of recent models is incomplete

Section 2 says the paper differs from recent majority-versus-unanimity models because it adds weak-state agenda setting and a dynamic delay channel, but the comparison stays mostly verbal. Appendix B.3 is useful, yet it still stops short of showing the benchmark fixed-proposer environments as literal special cases of the current notation. That matters because novelty claims in this part of the literature usually need a clean reduction argument, not just a discussion paragraph. Readers should be able to see exactly which objects are inherited from the one-shot models and which are new here. The paper would be more complete with a short appendix proposition that imposes one-shot bargaining, a fixed weak proposer, no entry, and no proposer randomization, and then computes majority and unanimity payoffs in the current symbols. That exercise should show explicitly that D, continuation-priced a_0(1), and the entry sets disappear, while the low-only versus pooling comparison survives as the inherited core.

Recommendation: major revision. The paper has a clear idea and a more careful formal architecture than many drafts in this area, especially in distinguishing assessment-dependent claims from the rest of the argument. Still, the core comparison currently relies too heavily on a maintained belief assessment and on a majority benchmark that builds in much of the institutional contrast, so I do not think the manuscript yet meets top-venue standards without stronger robustness, sharper primitive conditions, and a clearer empirical bridge.

Key revision targets:

  1. Rework the unanimity equilibrium section so the role of the weak-vote-passive assessment is either derived from a more primitive voting protocol or stress-tested against plausible alternative public-vote belief rules.
  2. Extend the institutional comparison beyond the full-surplus no-H majority benchmark by analyzing the rho < 1 case formally and by deriving primitive conditions for when Delta_H(mu) is positive on a nonempty both-form region.
  3. Add a bounded extension with pi_H > 0, such as neutral recognition and a hegemonic agenda-power case, to show whether the informational-pivotality mechanism survives once proposal power is reintroduced.
  4. Strengthen the empirical bridge by translating the P, L, and D candidates into observable implications and by showing how the model can be distinguished from agenda control or simple complete-information veto power in an OPEC-style setting.
  5. Either broaden the package technology or provide a tighter microfoundation for the linear fixed-pie specification so the screening result does not look like a consequence of a transfer-like normalization.

1. Delay candidate proof leaves the N=3 case unstated

Status: [Pending]

Quote: > if there is only one non-proposing weak > voter, any deviation that could make passage possible gives that voter zero rather than 𝑐(𝜇).

Feedback: This sentence is too quick for the full stated domain of the theorem. Once there are at least two non-proposing weak voters, the argument is fine because another weak rejection keeps the ballot failed after a unilateral yes-deviation. But Proposition 2 is stated for all N ≥ 3, and when N = 3 there is only one non-proposing weak voter. In that case the deviation payoff is not always zero: it is 0 if H pools on yes, μ c(1) under low-yes/high-no separation, (1-μ)c(0) under low-no/high-yes separation, and c(μ) if H pools on no. The result still goes through, since c(μ) = β p_2(μ)/m is at least μ c(1) and at least (1-μ)c(0), but the proof should actually show that comparison. A short N=3 paragraph here would close the gap cleanly and make the proposition proved on its full stated range rather than only in cases with at least two non-proposing weak voters.

2. Abstract overstates the majority benchmark

Status: [Pending]

Quote: > Under > majority rule, weak states avoid screening when the hegemon is not a cheaper coalition partner > than a weak voter.

Feedback: Section 5 and Appendix A.1 make the benchmark slightly tighter than this sentence suggests. The no-H path ties the low-only H-including path at a_0^M = c^M = β/m, so the selected no-screening benchmark needs the strict inequality a_0^M > β/m unless the paper adds a separate tie convention for the knife-edge. The body already handles this correctly, so this is an abstract-level tightening, not a change to the result. I would rewrite the sentence to say that majority avoids screening when the hegemon is strictly more costly to buy than a weak voter, with equality treated as a boundary case.
