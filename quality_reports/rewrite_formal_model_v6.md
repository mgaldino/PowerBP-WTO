## Abstract

Proposal rights are a well-documented source of political power. This paper asks whether a hegemon that holds none retains another: private information, converted into concessions by the voting rule. In a two-round bargaining game, only weak states propose, and a hegemon privately knows whether its disagreement payoff is low or high; majority and unanimity differ solely in the number of votes required. Under majority, a proposer can replace the hegemon with uninformed weak-state votes, and this substitute caps the value of its private information. Under unanimity, the hegemon's vote is an essential input and proposals must respond to a threshold weak states cannot observe. To separate this informational component from the value of a necessary vote, we benchmark each private game against its complete-information counterpart and compare the resulting type-specific informational rents across rules. The contrast is conditional. When the high type is sufficiently likely, it is positive for the low type whenever majority screens or excludes the hegemon with or without information, zero when majority already pays the pooling price, and of either sign when private majority excludes a low type that public majority would include; the high type gains only when private majority screens where public majority would exclude it. For intermediate beliefs, unanimity has no perfect Bayesian equilibrium in pure ballot strategies, so the comparison is undefined there. Private information can thus substitute for agenda control as a source of hegemonic power, but only where the voting rule removes substitutes for the informed vote.

## Introduction

# Introduction

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}

Saudi Arabia has no formal authority to write OPEC's production agreements,
yet an agreement that ignored its spare capacity and outside opportunities
would be difficult to sustain [@fattouh2013opec; @nakov2013saudi]. The World
Trade Organization presents the mirror image: the United States accepted a
consensus rule that gives every member an equal vote
[@steinberg2002shadow].^[For the model, *consensus* and *unanimity* denote
the same voting rule: every state must approve. The distinction between the
terms does not affect the analysis.] In both organizations a powerful state
holds neither exclusive proposal rights nor extra votes, and in both it knows
more than its partners about what it can obtain if no agreement is reached.
Where does its influence come from?

The standard answer is proposal rights. @kalandrakis2006proposal shows that
the right to decide what is put to a vote translates into political power,
and legislative-bargaining models since @baron1989bargaining have traced how
recognition rules and agenda control distribute surplus
[@winter1996voting; @eraslan2019legislative]. This paper removes that source
deliberately. The hegemon never proposes, and its vote counts as one among
many. The question then becomes whether any other source of power remains.
The answer we develop is that private information can be one, but only under
voting rules that make the informed vote impossible to replace.

The institutional version of the question is when unanimity advantages a
hegemon over majority rule, given equal votes and no hegemonic agenda
control, and how much of that advantage arises from private information
rather than from the veto that unanimity confers on everyone. The second half
matters because a necessary vote is valuable even when its holder's strength
is common knowledge. The informational puzzle begins where the
complete-information benchmark ends: what does a hegemon gain because the
other states cannot price its vote?

We study a two-round bargaining game with one hegemon and at least three weak
states. Only weak states propose. The hegemon privately knows whether its
terminal disagreement payoff is low or high. Proposals and simultaneous
ballots are public, the institutional surplus is fixed, and a failed first
round leads to a discounted terminal round. Majority and unanimity differ
only in the number of votes required.

The mechanism is coalition substitutability. Under majority, a weak-state
proposer can assemble a winning coalition from uninformed weak-state votes
and leave the hegemon out. That outside coalition caps the price of the
hegemon's vote, and with it the value of anything the hegemon privately
knows. Under unanimity the substitute disappears. Every agreement requires
the hegemon's approval, so the proposal must respond to a threshold that weak
states cannot observe. When the high type is sufficiently likely, the
proposer pays the high threshold to both types, and a low-type hegemon
collects the concession designed for a stronger one. The rent arises from
pooling: weak states price the hegemon as strong because a failed proposal
has no fallback coalition.

Comparing unanimity with majority does not by itself identify this rent. A
hegemon may receive more under unanimity simply because its vote has become
necessary, an advantage that exists under complete information. We therefore
solve each voting rule twice, with the hegemon's type public and private, and
define the informational rent of a rule as the difference between its private
and public payoffs for each type. The difference of these rents across rules
measures what unanimity adds to the return on private information once the
value of a necessary vote has been removed.

That measure is conditional in ways a single-number comparison would hide.
Majority has pure-strategy equilibria featuring exclusion, type-contingent
screening, or pooling, depending on outside options and beliefs. Unanimity
has an immediate low-type agreement when the low type is certain, an
immediate pooling agreement when the high type is sufficiently likely, and no
perfect Bayesian equilibrium in pure ballot strategies between these cases.
Above the pooling cutoff, the low type's informational advantage under
unanimity is positive when majority screens or when majority excludes the
hegemon with or without information, zero when majority already pools, and of
either sign when private majority excludes a low type that public majority
would include. The high type gains only when private majority screens where
public majority would exclude it, and loses when private majority excludes a
high type that public majority would include. Where private unanimity has no
pure-strategy equilibrium the contrast is empty; we report that emptiness as
a boundary of the equilibrium domain and do not fill it with a counterfactual
payoff.

Two recent papers also make disagreement values private and compare majority
with unanimity, through distinct mechanisms. @piazolo2025legislative study
dynamic signaling by privately informed responders: under unanimity,
rejection is a valuable signal of toughness, whereas majority disciplines
such signaling through the threat of exclusion, so votes are more expensive
under unanimity. @glynia2026unanimity shut down the dynamic channel and show
that uncertainty about several responders changes the proposer's choice
between insuring against costly types and gambling on cheap ones. In both
papers, private information is dispersed among symmetric responders and the
object of analysis is the price of votes or the probability of agreement.
@miller2018heterogeneous supply the complete-information benchmark with
heterogeneous disagreement values, in which an expensive player can be
included under unanimity and excluded under majority.

We ask a distributional question about one asymmetric actor, and three
features of the model separate it from this work. Private information is
concentrated in a hegemon that never proposes, so any rent it earns cannot be
attributed to agenda power. The voting rule determines whether other votes
substitute for the informed one, and substitutability determines whether
private information can be converted into concessions at all. And the
complete-information benchmark for each rule separates the informational
component of unanimity's advantage from its veto component. The threat of
exclusion in @piazolo2025legislative and the coalition substitute here can be
read as the same institutional fact from two sides: they trace its
consequences for responders' incentives to signal, we trace its consequences
for the rents of the actor whose vote the rule makes replaceable or
essential. Rational-design and informal-power accounts of international
institutions [@koremenos2001rational; @steinberg2002shadow;
@stone2011controlling] document influence that formal rules do not record;
the model specifies one channel for that influence and the voting rules under
which it operates.
