# Rewritten Introduction: Informational Power Through Pivotality

**Original file**: `/Users/manoelgaldino/Documents/DCP/Papers/PowerBayesianPersuasion/formal_model_v5.Rmd`  
**Date**: 2026-05-15

---

Power in international institutions often appears to follow proposal rights. Actors who draft agendas, write institutional packages, or command winning coalitions seem best positioned to extract concessions. Many institutions give powerful actors a different source of leverage: a position that other actors cannot bypass. Consensus rules, veto systems, and weighted-voting arrangements can make a powerful actor's approval necessary even when that actor does not formally write the proposal.

That position matters when the pivotal actor is privately informed. Weak states may observe the institutional package on the table, but not the participation threshold of the actor whose approval is needed. If a low-threshold type would accept modest concessions while a high-threshold type requires more, the weak coalition faces a screening problem. To secure agreement from both types, weak states may offer a package that gives the low-threshold type more than it needed to participate. Pivotality turns private participation thresholds into informational rents.

This paper develops a formal model of informational power through pivotality. The model deliberately removes agenda power from the powerful actor. The hegemon has zero recognition probability, \(\pi_H=0\), and only weak states make proposals. Any advantage the hegemon obtains must therefore come from being privately informed and institutionally necessary, not from controlling the agenda. Proposals are fixed-pie institutional packages, such as quota shares, production flexibility, monitoring provisions, enforcement burdens, or exemptions. A more generous package benefits the hegemon and reduces the residual surplus available to the weak coalition.

The institutional comparison between majority and unanimity isolates the mechanism. Majority rule is a benchmark in which weak states can exclude the hegemon when the hegemon is not a cheaper coalition partner than another weak voter. In that case, the hegemon's private threshold does not create a screening problem for the weak coalition. Unanimity represents institutional arrangements in which the hegemon cannot be bypassed. The same logic applies to veto systems and weighted-voting environments where formal passage or practical implementation requires the participation of a powerful actor. When the hegemon is pivotal, weak proposers must decide whether to pool types with a generous package, test the low-threshold type with a tougher package, or delay.

The model produces four results. First, majority removes screening only under a substantive No-Cheap-H condition: the low-threshold hegemon must not be cheaper to buy than a weak voter. If this condition fails, majority can also use the hegemon as a cheap coalition partner. Second, under unanimity, terminal bargaining has a threshold structure. Weak proposers use a low-threshold package when the high type is unlikely and a pooling package when the high type is sufficiently likely. Third, in Round 1, under the weak-vote-passive assessment, the selected PBE outcome is payoff-equivalent to one of three candidates: pooling, low-only acceptance, or no-information rejection. The result is a selection claim under the stated public-voting assessment, not a uniqueness result over unrestricted PBEs. Fourth, because majority preserves the weak coalition's full surplus whenever No-Cheap-H holds, weak-state entry under unanimity is nested in entry under majority.

The comparison therefore gives a conditional theory of when institutional non-excludability benefits a powerful informed actor. If neither rule forms, the hegemon is indifferent. If only majority forms, majority is better for the hegemon. If both rules form, the hegemon's ranking is determined by the payoff gap \(\Delta_H(\mu)\), which compares the pivotality rent under unanimity with the payoff from exclusion under majority. In the working OPEC-style illustration, unanimity forms for entry costs up to \(0.062\), majority forms up to \(0.083\), and the hegemon prefers unanimity when the probability of the high-threshold state is below \(0.7\).

The contribution is to separate three sources of institutional power that are often bundled together: outside-option power, proposal power, and pivotality. Self-binding accounts explain why powerful states may accept constraints to make cooperation credible. Informal-power accounts show how powerful states can dominate outcomes by controlling drafting, procedures, or outside options. Bargaining models show how proposal rights shape distributive outcomes. This paper identifies a source of power that remains when proposal power is assigned to weak states. A powerful actor can extract rents because its privately known participation threshold becomes a constraint on every feasible agreement.

The mechanism is useful for studying organizations in which formal equality coexists with asymmetric power. OPEC is one such setting. Smaller producers may participate formally as equals and may bargain over institutional packages such as quotas, flexibility, monitoring, and enforcement. Yet a package that cannot secure Saudi Arabia's participation may be politically ineffective. The model does not claim that Saudi Arabia writes every package, and the numerical exercise is not an empirical calibration. It shows how a pivotal producer's private participation threshold can make consensus valuable to that producer even when formal agenda control lies elsewhere.

This perspective changes the interpretation of consensus and related institutional rules. Consensus need not protect only weak states, and majority need not favor the powerful actor. The institutional effect depends on whether the rule lets weak states bypass the informed powerful actor. When they can bypass it, private thresholds lose distributive force. When they cannot, pivotality makes information politically productive.
