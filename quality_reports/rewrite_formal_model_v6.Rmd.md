# Introduction

In the years after the Cold War, at the height of American power, the United
States led the creation of the World Trade Organization. The institution rested
on rules of sovereign equality: each member held one formal vote, decisions were
normally taken by consensus, and no constitutive rule gave Washington an
exclusive right to place proposals before the membership. The institutional
form leveled authority just as power in the international system became more
unequal. Yet bargaining outcomes in the trading system continued to reflect the
asymmetry between the United States and most other members. Why would a hegemon
help create an organization in which every state could withhold its approval?

Steinberg's account of the GATT and WTO gives the canonical answer
[@steinberg2002shadow]. Formal equality coexisted with an informal weighting of
influence. The United States and the European Community shaped agendas and
drafts, used market access and exit options to alter other states' alternatives,
assembled linked packages, and gathered information through successive
proposals and objections. At the close of the Uruguay Round, they paired the
single undertaking with withdrawal from GATT 1947 after joining the WTO. States
that rejected the new package could no longer rely on the old transatlantic
trading relationship as their fallback. This episode shows how material power
can enter a formally egalitarian institution through the agenda, the contract
space, and the value of disagreement.

It also leaves a sharper counterfactual. Suppose the familiar agenda advantage
is removed. Can the approval rule itself convert an underlying asymmetry into a
distributive advantage for the hegemon? We study a benchmark in which the
hegemon has one vote, never proposes, and privately knows whether its payoff if
negotiations fail is low or high. Weak states control proposal rights. This is an
identifying counterfactual, not a description of the full WTO bargaining
process. It holds agenda power fixed to isolate what consensus does when the
hegemon's willingness to accept cannot be observed directly.

The answer turns on coalition substitutes. Under majority rule, a weak proposer
can replace the hegemon with another weak-state vote. That outside coalition
caps the price of the hegemon's approval and can remove its private information
from the relevant bargain. Under unanimity, there is no substitute. Every
agreement requires the hegemon's approval, so weak states must choose a package
without knowing the reservation value of an indispensable participant. When the
high type is sufficiently likely, they offer the high threshold to both types.
The low type then receives a concession designed for a stronger hegemon. Its
rent comes from pooling around an approval that cannot be replaced.

Unanimity can also raise the hegemon's payoff when its type is publicly known,
simply because a necessary vote commands a price. A comparison of private games
would confound that veto value with the return on private information. We
therefore solve each approval rule under public and private information. For
each type and rule, informational rent is the private payoff minus the public
payoff at the same parameter point. The difference of these rents across rules
identifies the part of unanimity's distributive effect that arises because weak
states cannot price the hegemon's vote.

The resulting comparison is conditional. Majority may exclude the hegemon,
screen its types, or pool them, depending on disagreement values and beliefs.
Unanimity yields immediate low-type agreement when the low type is certain and
immediate pooling when the high type is sufficiently likely. In the
intermediate-belief region, the unanimity game has no perfect Bayesian
equilibrium in pure ballot strategies under the paper's declared belief and
voting disciplines. We preserve that empty correspondence and assign it no
counterfactual payoff.

Where both private games have a pure-strategy equilibrium, the incidence of
informational power is type-specific. Above the unanimity pooling cutoff, the
low type gains when majority screens, gains or loses under exclusion according
to whether the discounted high threshold exceeds its current disagreement
payoff, and receives no additional rent when majority already pools. The high
type's informational-rent contrast changes only when private information alters
its treatment under majority. Consensus can therefore benefit a hegemon through
private information, but neither every type nor every parameter region benefits.

The paper builds on two strands of bargaining theory. Baron and Ferejohn place
proposal and voting rules inside a sequential bargaining game
[@baron1989bargaining], and Kalandrakis shows how recognition probabilities can
turn proposal rights into political power [@kalandrakis2006proposal]. We remove
the hegemon's access to that instrument. Work on heterogeneous disagreement
values establishes the complete-information benchmark in which costly players
are protected by unanimity and may be excluded by majority
[@miller2018heterogeneous]. The closest incomplete-information papers identify
two further mechanisms. Piazolo and Vanberg show that rejection can signal a
high disagreement value in a dynamic game [@piazolo2025legislative]. Glynia,
Thum, and Xefteris study a one-shot choice between insuring against costly
responders and gambling on cheaper votes [@glynia2026unanimity]. Our setting
concentrates private information in one asymmetric actor and lets the voting
rule determine whether any informed vote must enter the coalition. The separate
literature section develops these relationships and gives full credit to the
mechanisms established by the neighboring papers.

The model section defines the bargaining protocol and solution concept. The
results then solve the public benchmarks, the private terminal and first-round
games, and the type-specific informational rents. The discussion returns to the
creation of the WTO, the GATT fallback, and the observable distinction between
pooling around an indispensable vote and signaling through visible rejection.
The appendices report the proofs, endpoint conventions, exact correspondences,
and worked values.
