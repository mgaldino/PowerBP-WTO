# Common voting protocol for unanimity and majority

Date: 2026-05-11

Status: adopted protocol assessment before deriving R1.

## Correction

An earlier protocol note considered a required-coalition consent convention.
That is superseded. The common protocol should stay close to standard
Baron-Ferejohn voting and should not suppress votes by actors outside the
winning coalition.

## Common protocol

Use simultaneous public voting with ex post vote records:

```text
1. A proposer is recognized according to the recognition protocol.
2. The proposer makes a proposal and is counted as voting yes.
3. All other states vote simultaneously.
4. Individual votes are publicly revealed after all ballots are cast.
5. The proposal passes if the number of yes votes reaches the institutional
   quota.
6. Unanimity uses quota N.
7. Majority uses quota q = floor(N/2) + 1.
8. Players accept in indifference.
```

Equivalently, voting can be interpreted as a secret ballot with each player's
vote opened and recorded after the ballot closes.

The public history after the ballot includes the proposal, the full vote vector,
and the outcome. If the game continues, beliefs are updated from that public
history.

## Why simultaneity matters

In standard symmetric Baron-Ferejohn models, the order of votes is usually not
substantive. Here it is substantive because `H` is privately informed and may be
pivotal under one institution but not the other.

Simultaneous voting avoids making the order of votes a hidden source of
information. `H` cannot condition its vote on whether weak states have already
assembled a winning coalition, and weak states cannot condition their votes on
`H`'s vote inside the same ballot. Information is revealed only after the vote
record is public.

## Pivotality, not ballot access

The informational distinction should come from pivotality, not from whether a
vote is recorded.

Under unanimity:

```text
H is pivotal because every state must vote yes.
If H votes no, the proposal fails.
If H's vote differs by type, the ex post vote record is informative.
```

Under majority:

```text
A weak proposer can potentially assemble q weak yes votes without H.
H's vote is still recorded.
But if q weak states support the proposal, H's vote is not pivotal.
```

Thus majority does not mechanically hide `H`'s information. The proof must show
that an optimal majority proposal does not make `H` pivotal.

## Important consequence for majority

Under majority, a weak proposer needs q yes votes. Since the proposer votes yes,
it needs k = q - 1 additional yes votes.

A no-H majority package buys k weak votes. An H-including package can instead
try to buy H's vote and only k - 1 weak votes.

Therefore no-screening under majority is not automatic from the quota alone. It
requires that `H` is not a cheaper coalition partner than a weak voter.

In the pi_H = 0, fixed-pie baseline, let:

```text
m = N - 1
q = floor(N/2) + 1
k = q - 1
W2_M = 1/m
c_M = beta W2_M = beta/m
a_theta_M = beta C_H2_M(theta) - b_H(theta)
```

Here c_M is the public continuation value that must be paid to a weak voter in
R1 majority, and a_theta_M is H's R1 acceptance threshold if the proposer tries
to use H as a majority coalition member.

A sufficient no-screening condition is:

```text
a_0_M >= c_M
```

Because a_1_M > a_0_M, this also rules out buying both H types as a cheaper
substitute for a weak voter.

If this condition fails, majority may itself have a screening region: the weak
proposer may prefer to buy low H's vote and one fewer weak vote. That would be a
real feature of the model, not a protocol artifact.

## Consequences for R1 majority

Under pi_H = 0 and the no-cheap-H condition:

```text
H is not a proposer.
The weak proposer forms a weak-state winning coalition.
The optimal concession to H is y = 0.
H may vote no in both types.
The vote record is public but pooling in H's type on the equilibrium path.
Weak voters' acceptance constraints are public and do not depend on H's private
type.
```

Therefore majority remains the no-screening comparison, but this now rests on a
substantive cost condition: `H` is not a cheaper vote than a weak coalition
partner.

## Consequences for R1 unanimity

Under unanimity:

```text
H cannot be excluded.
Any accepted agreement must satisfy H's type-dependent participation threshold.
H's simultaneous vote is payoff-relevant because one no vote defeats the
proposal.
The ex post vote record can reveal H's type when low and high H vote
differently.
```

This is where private information creates informational rents.

## Agenda-power extension

The pi_H > 0 extension should use the same simultaneous public voting protocol.
The difficult subgames are not generated by vote order; they are generated by
H's proposals potentially signaling type when rejection leads to future
bargaining.

R2 with H proposal power is likely simpler than R1: in a final-period
ultimatum, H buys the required weak votes at their public outside values and
keeps the residual. R1 with H proposal power remains the hard signaling case
because rejection leads to a future subgame whose value may depend on H's type.

## Recommended baseline

Use simultaneous public BF-style voting with ex post vote records:

```text
The same ballot protocol applies under unanimity and majority.
Only the quota changes.
Unanimity uses quota N.
Majority uses quota q = floor(N/2) + 1.
The proposer is counted as voting yes.
All other votes are simultaneous and publicly revealed after the ballot.
```

This preserves comparability across institutions and keeps the mechanism tied
to pivotality rather than to an arbitrary order of voting.
