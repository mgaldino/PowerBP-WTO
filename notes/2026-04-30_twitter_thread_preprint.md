New preprint: "Informational Power Through Pivotality: When a Hegemon May Choose Consensus"

Why would a powerful state accept consensus in an international organization — giving every member a veto — instead of imposing majority rule, where it could build coalitions and exclude rivals?

🔗 https://osf.io/preprints/socarxiv/ca8vj_v3

**2/**
The standard answer: the hegemon either constrains itself to build credibility (self-binding) or wields informal power regardless of formal rules (shadow of power).

Neither explains WHY the hegemon would choose this rule in the first place. If it uses informal power anyway, why not just impose majority?

**3/**
My answer: consensus is not a concession by the powerful — it's an institutional technology of power. Here's the mechanism.

Under unanimity, every agreement requires the hegemon's vote. Weaker states must decide how much to offer the hegemon WITHOUT knowing how valuable cooperation really is.

**4/**
This uncertainty creates a screening problem. If weak states lowball the hegemon, they risk rejection. If they offer too much, they overpay. The switch between these strategies generates a discrete jump in the hegemon's expected payoff — a screening rent.

**5/**
Under majority rule, weak states can form winning coalitions WITHOUT the hegemon. No need for the hegemon's vote → no screening problem → no informational rent. The hegemon's private information becomes useless.

Paradoxically, knowing more makes you MORE excludable under majority.

**6/**
The institutional comparison has a clean structure:
- Unanimity dominates wherever it can sustain participation
- Majority's only advantage is wider institutional viability (it lowers the bar for entry)
- Conditional on entry, unanimity ALWAYS gives the hegemon more

**7/**
In an OPEC calibration (Saudi Arabia as hegemon, ~1986), the hegemon captures 28% of the cooperation surplus under unanimity vs 23% under majority — a 5 percentage-point screening premium that majority eliminates entirely.

**8/**
Recent OPEC events fit the model's predictions. Angola (2024) and UAE (2026) left as their outside options improved — exactly when the model predicts unanimity stops being individually rational for members. OPEC responded in 2027 by commissioning independent capacity audits — trying to reduce the informational asymmetry that sustains screening rents.

**9/**
A discriminating prediction: consensus should be most common where the value of cooperation is hardest to assess (regulatory bodies, standard-setters) and absent where stakes are transparent (financial institutions with weighted voting). This matches the cross-section documented by Gould (2016).

**10/**
The formal results use Baron-Ferejohn legislative bargaining with asymmetric information and entry costs. Proofs cover binary and continuous type spaces. The screening rent is distribution-free: it's strictly positive under unanimity for ANY distribution and zero under majority.

**11/**
This grew out of a puzzle from my MA thesis 15 years ago — why the US accepted consensus at the WTO. I couldn't solve it then. The tools I needed (screening under pivotality in bargaining models) weren't available yet.

Submitted to Review of International Organizations.
