# Technical Note: The Empirical Case for BP Commitment in the GATT/WTO

**Date**: 2026-04-21  
**Purpose**: Working document on the empirical plausibility of Bayesian Persuasion commitment in international trade negotiations. Intended to support a new subsection in the paper (before the formal model) and to preempt the predictable referee objection: "Why BP and not cheap talk or evidence games?"

---

## 1. The Problem

The paper's central mechanism relies on Bayesian Persuasion (BP), which assumes the hegemon can commit to an information structure *before* observing the state of the world. This is the paper's most exposed assumption. A skeptical referee will argue that (a) states cannot credibly commit to disclosure rules, (b) cheap talk is more realistic in diplomatic settings, and (c) the results might not survive under weaker informational assumptions.

The current paper addresses this in the Scope section (Section 9) with three sentences: reputation, institutional transparency rules, and an upper-bound interpretation. This is too thin and too late. The defense reads as an afterthought when it should be a substantive part of the paper's setup.

This note organizes the empirical evidence around three mechanisms that, taken together, constitute a credible commitment technology for information design in the GATT/WTO context.

---

## 2. Three Commitment Mechanisms

### 2A. Selective Commissioning and Release of Economic Studies (= BP in Practice)

**This is the strongest evidence.** It maps directly onto the formal structure of Bayesian Persuasion: the hegemon chooses an information structure (which models to commission, which scenarios to publicize) *before* the state of the world is fully realized, and the resulting signals shape the beliefs of weaker states about the value of cooperation.

**The Uruguay Round projections.** Before and during the Uruguay Round (1986--1994), the United States Trade Representative (USTR) actively promoted economic projections of the gains from trade liberalization. These projections were produced by the World Bank, the OECD, and GATT's own research division. The most widely cited figures estimated global welfare gains of $235--274 billion annually from a successful round. As UNCTAD documented, these numbers were "widely advertised by GATT, OECD and World Bank to put pressure on countries to conclude the Round" (UNCTAD 1996, Trade and Development Report, p. 143). The projections were not neutral forecasts; they were strategic communications designed to shift beliefs about the value of cooperation.

Critically, these projections were later shown to be substantially inflated. Stiglitz and Charlton (2005) argue in *Fair Trade for All* that the headline figures misrepresented the likely distribution of gains, overstating benefits to developing countries while understating adjustment costs. The actual welfare effects, particularly for the poorest countries, fell well short of the projections. This pattern---optimistic ex ante projections that served the hegemon's negotiating interests---is precisely what BP predicts. The hegemon did not fabricate data; it chose which models to run, which assumptions to highlight, and which scenarios to publicize. The choice of information structure was the instrument of persuasion.

**The mechanism is anticipatory, not reactive.** This distinction is important for the BP interpretation. USTR did not wait to observe the outcome and then spin it; it commissioned studies and circulated projections *before* the key negotiating phases, shaping the informational environment within which weaker states formed beliefs and made entry decisions. The modeling choices (CGE models with full employment assumptions, static gains emphasized over dynamic costs, aggregate figures rather than distributional breakdowns) were made ex ante. This maps onto BP's timing: the sender commits to a signal structure before the state is realized.

**Steinberg (2002)**, already in the paper's bibliography, documents how the US exercised what he calls "invisible weighting" and power-based bargaining within the consensus framework of the GATT/WTO. His account emphasizes the US capacity to set the analytical framing of negotiations---determining which issues were on the table, which baselines were used for comparison, and which projections were treated as authoritative. This is information design operating through institutional channels.

**Strength of evidence**: HIGH. The Uruguay Round projections are well-documented, the gap between projections and outcomes is established in the literature, and the mechanism maps directly onto BP's formal structure.

### 2B. Intelligence Gathering as Observing theta

The BP model assumes the hegemon privately observes the state theta---the true value of multilateral cooperation. For this assumption to be empirically grounded, the hegemon must have systematically superior information about the value of cooperation, the reservation values of other states, and the distributional consequences of proposed agreements. The evidence on economic espionage in trade negotiations establishes that this informational advantage was actively cultivated and was not merely a byproduct of capacity differences.

**The ECHELON system and the Uruguay Round.** The European Parliament's 2001 report on the ECHELON surveillance system (Report A5-0264/2001, Rapporteur: Gerhard Schmid) compiled evidence that the United States and its Five Eyes partners operated a global signals intelligence network that intercepted diplomatic and commercial communications. The report specifically noted concerns that "Americans used information obtained from hacking to help them in negotiations on GATT." While the report's conclusions were necessarily hedged (intelligence agencies do not confirm operations), the European Parliament found the evidence credible enough to recommend countermeasures.

**CIA economic espionage operations (1993).** During the final phase of the Uruguay Round, the CIA's Paris station conducted economic espionage operations specifically targeting French negotiating positions. This was not an isolated initiative. CIA Director R. James Woolsey publicly declared economic intelligence a priority for the post-Cold War era, writing in the *Wall Street Journal* in 1995 that economic espionage against allies was a legitimate intelligence function. The targeting of Uruguay Round negotiations with France is documented in journalistic and declassified sources.

**Snowden/WikiLeaks revelations.** Documents released by Edward Snowden and published by WikiLeaks revealed that the National Security Agency (NSA) monitored Japanese government deliberations on trade policy, including positions being developed for the Doha Round. Similar surveillance targeted other negotiating partners.

**What this establishes.** These cases do not prove that the US had perfect information about theta. They establish something more important for the model's plausibility: the hegemon *invested heavily* in acquiring private information about the value of cooperation and the negotiating positions of other states. The model's assumption that H observes theta while W does not is a stylized representation of a real and documented informational asymmetry that was actively maintained through intelligence operations, not merely inherited from capacity differences.

**Strength of evidence**: MODERATE-HIGH. The ECHELON report and Woolsey's public statements are on the record. The specific operational details are less well-documented, as is typical for intelligence matters. The Snowden revelations provide additional corroboration for the post-2000 period. The evidence is strongest as support for the *direction* of informational asymmetry (the hegemon invests in observing theta) rather than for its *magnitude* (perfect observation).

### 2C. Structural Information Asymmetry --- The Precondition

Even without espionage, the structural distribution of analytical capacity in the multilateral trading system generates a persistent informational asymmetry. This is the precondition that makes both commissioning (2A) and intelligence (2B) strategically valuable.

**Delegation size and composition.** At the 2001 Doha Ministerial Conference, delegation sizes varied dramatically: the EU brought 508 delegates, Japan 159, the United States 51, while Haiti sent 1 (Bown and Hoekman 2005). These numbers reflect not just diplomatic presence but analytical capacity: the ability to follow parallel negotiating tracks, evaluate technical proposals, and assess the implications of draft texts in real time.

**Legal and analytical capacity.** Busch, Reinhardt, and Shaffer (2009) construct a legal capacity index for WTO members and show that developing countries face severe constraints in their ability to participate effectively in the dispute settlement system and, by extension, in understanding the implications of proposed rules. Their data show that many developing countries lack the specialized trade lawyers and economists needed to independently evaluate the consequences of complex regulatory texts.

**Permanent mission staffing.** Page (2003), in work for the Overseas Development Institute, documented the average size of WTO permanent missions: developed countries averaged 7.38 staff members compared to 3.51 for developing countries. Given that the WTO's daily work involves simultaneous meetings across multiple committees, smaller delegations cannot cover all negotiations, let alone independently analyze the proposals being discussed.

**The TRIPS case: private sector as information designer.** Drahos and Braithwaite (2002), based on more than 500 interviews, document how the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS) was designed. The Intellectual Property Committee (IPC)---a coalition of 12 US CEOs from companies including Pfizer, IBM, and Monsanto---drafted the framework that became TRIPS. Sell (2003) provides a complementary account in *Private Power, Public Law*, showing how private actors with deep technical knowledge shaped the informational environment in which governments negotiated. Developing countries, as Jawara and Kwa (2003) document in *Behind the Scenes at the WTO*, were often presented with draft texts in Green Room sessions with limited time to assess their implications. Multiple sources, including UNCTAD documents, note that "developing countries were not adequately prepared and had only partial awareness of the full implications" of what they were agreeing to.

**What this establishes.** The structural asymmetry means that the preconditions for BP are met: there is a persistent informational gap between the hegemon (and its private-sector allies) and weaker states. The hegemon's information advantage is not merely about knowing theta in isolation; it extends to understanding the *implications* of theta for different parties---precisely the kind of information that, when selectively disclosed, shifts beliefs about the value of cooperation.

**Strength of evidence**: HIGH. The delegation data are publicly available and uncontested. The TRIPS case is exhaustively documented. The analytical capacity gap is a consensus finding in the trade governance literature.

## 3. Theoretical Defense (Brief)

The empirical evidence above supports the plausibility of BP commitment, but the paper should also address the theoretical objection directly.

**The qualitative result is structural.** The core finding---that unanimity creates a screening non-convexity absent under majority---does not depend on BP. The screening cutoff exists whenever there is incomplete information under unanimity. BP quantifies the hegemon's gain from exploiting the non-convexity, but the non-convexity itself is a property of the voting rule interacting with informational asymmetry.

**BP as an upper bound.** Even if full commitment is unavailable, the BP payoff represents the maximum value of the hegemon's informational advantage under any communication protocol. The institutional comparison (unanimity vs. majority) remains valid as long as the hegemon can extract *some* informational rent from the screening structure. BP gives the cleanest closed-form expressions, but the qualitative ranking would survive under weaker assumptions.

**Commitment is about structure, not content.** In the GATT/WTO context, BP commitment means committing to the *type* of analysis to commission (which models, which scenarios, which baselines)---not to a specific numerical claim about theta. This is easier to sustain than it might initially appear. A government that has publicly funded a World Bank study cannot easily suppress its release; a USTR that has announced a modeling exercise must eventually publish results. The institutional infrastructure of economic research and policy analysis provides a natural commitment technology.

**Cheap talk comparison.** Under cheap talk (Crawford and Sobel 1982), the equilibrium involves partition signaling: the hegemon can convey coarse information but cannot achieve the fine-tuned belief manipulation of BP. However, even under cheap talk, the screening non-convexity under unanimity creates a relative informational advantage compared to majority rule, where the value function is linear regardless of the communication protocol. The gap between BP and cheap talk payoffs is about the *magnitude* of the informational advantage, not its *existence*. The partition equilibrium under cheap talk is coarser, but the structural comparison (unanimity enables screening, majority does not) is preserved.

**Evidence games.** Under verifiable disclosure (Milgrom 1981, Grossman 1981), the hegemon can prove claims about theta but cannot fabricate evidence. This intermediate case---between cheap talk and full BP---is arguably the most realistic. The hegemon can choose what to reveal (which studies to commission, which results to highlight) but cannot lie about what the studies show. This is weaker than BP commitment but stronger than cheap talk, and the screening non-convexity under unanimity remains exploitable.

## 4. Suggested Placement in the Paper

### Why before the formal model, not in the Scope section

The current paper addresses BP commitment in the Scope section (Section 9), after the model, results, and GATT/WTO illustration. This placement has two problems:

1. **It reads as defensive.** By the time a reader reaches Section 9, they have either accepted the BP assumption or flagged it as a concern. Addressing it after the fact feels like an afterthought---a concession that the assumption is problematic rather than a confident assertion that it is empirically grounded.

2. **The formal model appears unmotivated.** Without prior discussion of how information design works in practice, the BP assumption looks like a modeling convenience rather than a substantively motivated choice. Readers unfamiliar with BP may wonder why the paper does not use cheap talk or unverifiable communication, which are more standard in IPE models.

### Recommended placement

**Option A (preferred): New subsection at the end of Section 2**, titled "Information Design in Practice" or "Empirical Foundations of Commitment." Section 2 ("Consensus and Informational Power in International Organizations") currently ends at line 85 with a paragraph about informational asymmetry in trade negotiations. A new subsection here would:
- Flow naturally from the existing discussion of informational power
- Ground the BP assumption before the formal model is introduced
- Allow the reader to enter the model with a clear picture of *how* the hegemon designs information in practice

**Option B: Brief standalone Section 2.5** between the current Section 2 and Section 3 (Institutional Comparison), titled "The Practice of Information Design." This creates slightly more separation but signals that the empirical grounding is a first-class contribution, not a subsection of the theory discussion.

### Suggested length for the paper version

The paper version should be **400--600 words** (roughly 1--1.5 paragraphs of dense text or 3--4 shorter paragraphs). It should:
- Lead with the commissioning mechanism (2A) as the most direct BP analog
- Mention the structural capacity asymmetry (2C) briefly, since Section 2 already covers it
- Reference the intelligence evidence (2B) in a footnote or a single sentence
- Close with the theoretical defense (upper bound + cheap talk robustness) in 2--3 sentences
- Move the current Scope section's three-sentence defense into a cross-reference to this earlier discussion

## 7. Summary Assessment

| Mechanism | BP Mapping | Evidence Strength | Paper Role |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|------------|
| Commissioning studies (2A) | Direct: choosing signal structure ex ante | HIGH | Lead example |
| Intelligence gathering (2B) | Supports: H observes theta | MODERATE-HIGH | Supporting footnote |
| Structural asymmetry (2C) | Precondition: W does not observe theta | HIGH | Brief mention (already in Section 2) |

The combined evidence makes a strong case that BP commitment is not merely a convenient assumption but a reasonable stylization of documented practices in multilateral trade negotiations. The commissioning mechanism (2A) is the strongest individual piece because it maps most directly onto BP's formal structure: the hegemon chooses what analysis to produce and publicize before the negotiating state is fully realized. The intelligence evidence (2B) supports the informational asymmetry assumption. The structural evidence (2C) establishes the precondition.

The theoretical defense (upper bound + cheap talk robustness) provides the fallback: even if the reader is not fully persuaded that BP commitment holds, the qualitative institutional comparison survives under weaker assumptions. This two-pronged approach---empirical plausibility *plus* theoretical robustness---should satisfy all but the most committed skeptics.
