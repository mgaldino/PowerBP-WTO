# Historical Cases: Screening Under Unanimity/Consensus in International Organizations

**Date**: 2026-04-27  
**Purpose**: Identify 5-8 candidate cases where the paper's core mechanism (screening rent from informational asymmetry under unanimity/consensus) is documented in historical negotiations.

**The mechanism to look for**:
1. Hegemon (H) has private information about the value of cooperation (theta)
2. Unanimity/consensus rule forces weak states (W) to include H
3. W face uncertainty about H's reservation value
4. W make "conservative offers" -- conceding MORE than necessary to secure H's agreement
5. H benefits from this screening rent / overpayment
6. Under majority rule, W could bypass H, eliminating this informational advantage

---

## CASE 1: GATT/WTO -- US/EC Under Consensus (STRONGEST)

### Organization & Episode
Multiple GATT rounds (1947-1994) and WTO (1995-present), all operating under de facto consensus rule.

### Players
- **H**: United States (and to a lesser extent, the EC/EU)
- **W**: Developing countries, smaller trading nations

### theta (private information)
The true value the US places on specific tariff concessions -- i.e., how much domestic political cost the US bears from liberalizing a given sector, or equivalently, how valuable market access in a given sector truly is to the US economy. The US has superior knowledge of its own industry cost structures, political economy constraints, and reservation prices for specific product negotiations.

### alpha (outside option)
Market size: The US can credibly threaten to walk away from multilateral negotiations because its large domestic market gives it a strong unilateral outside option. Developing countries NEED access to US markets far more than the US needs access to theirs.

### Evidence of screening / overpayment / conservative offers

**Steinberg (2002, IO)** provides the most direct evidence. Key findings:
- Consensus decision-making at the GATT/WTO is "organized hypocrisy" -- formally egalitarian but substantively asymmetric.
- When bargaining is "power-based" (round closings), states bring "instruments of power that are extrinsic to rules, invisibly weighting the process and generating consensus outcomes that are asymmetrical."
- **Critical**: "The decision making rules have been maintained because they help generate information used by powerful states in the agenda-setting process." This is almost exactly the paper's mechanism in reverse -- the consensus rule serves as an information-generation device.
- The US and EC dominated agenda-setting through "green room" processes where developing countries were excluded from key negotiations but still had to consent to the final package.
- The Uruguay Round's "single undertaking" forced developing countries to accept TRIPS, TRIMs, and GATS as a package -- they "did not realize the full implication" of what they were accepting (documented in UNCTAD reports and G77 statements).

**Bagwell, Staiger & Yurukoglu (2020, AEJ: Applied)** used declassified GATT bargaining records from the Torquay Round (1950-51) to study bilateral tariff negotiations. The data shows product-level offer/counteroffer sequences that reveal information asymmetries in real negotiation.

**Green Room evidence**: The WTO "green room" process (Jones 2009; Patel 2007) shows how informational asymmetry operates in practice. The US had 360+ delegates at ministerial conferences; countries like Burundi had fewer than 4. Many developing countries had no permanent Geneva representation, meaning they could not monitor or participate in the information-rich informal negotiation process.

### Key Sources
- Steinberg, R.H. (2002). "In the Shadow of Law or Power? Consensus-Based Bargaining and Outcomes in the GATT/WTO." *International Organization* 56(2): 339-374.
- Bagwell, K., R.W. Staiger, and A. Yurukoglu (2020). "Multilateral Trade Bargaining: A First Look at the GATT Bargaining Records." *AEJ: Applied* 12(3): 72-105.
- Irwin, D.A., P.C. Mavroidis, and A.O. Sykes (2008). *The Genesis of the GATT*. Cambridge University Press.
- Jones, K. (2009). "Green room politics and the WTO's crisis of representation." *Progress in Development Studies* 9(4): 349-357.

### Evidence Strength: STRONG
Steinberg's "organized hypocrisy" framing maps closely to the model. The connection to INFORMATION GENERATION is explicitly stated. Bagwell-Staiger-Yurukoglu provides archival bargaining data. The weakness is that the mechanism Steinberg emphasizes is more about POWER-based information extraction than pure screening -- the US's informational advantage comes more from market size and credible threats than from private valuation of cooperation per se.

## CASE 2: Bretton Woods / IMF Creation (1944) (STRONG)

### Organization & Episode
The 1944 Bretton Woods Conference, establishing the IMF and World Bank. 44 countries participated, but negotiations were dominated by the US (White Plan) and UK (Keynes Plan).

### Players
- **H**: United States (holder of 60%+ of world gold reserves, sole major economy not devastated by war)
- **W**: United Kingdom, other European countries, Latin American nations

### theta (private information)
The true US willingness to provide liquidity to the postwar monetary system, and the terms under which the US would participate. White understood the US domestic political constraints and the precise value the US placed on dollar centrality. Keynes and other delegations had to guess at these.

### alpha (outside option)
Extremely high: The US held three-quarters of the world's monetary gold. It could credibly threaten autarky or bilateral arrangements. Britain, by contrast, was bankrupt and dependent on Lend-Lease aid. As Steil (2013) notes: "A bankrupt supplicant facing a monopolist of the world's acceptable cash holds bad cards indeed."

### Evidence of screening / overpayment / conservative offers

**Steil (2013, *The Battle of Bretton Woods*)** provides detailed archival evidence:
- White deliberately inserted "gold and U.S. dollars" (replacing "gold") into the final text, establishing dollar hegemony. Keynes "confessed that he did not read the final version of the document he signed." This is an extreme case of informational manipulation.
- White hosted a pre-conference drafting session at Atlantic City with 15 delegations, where "he learned which aspects of his proposal were likely to encounter opposition" -- i.e., he extracted information from W states before the formal negotiations.
- The conference operated by "negotiation and informal consensus rather than by formal voting" -- the consensus norm was crucial.
- The final agreement "resembled the White plan with some concessions in response to Keynes's concerns" -- the UK got far less than its Keynes Plan proposed (larger Fund, symmetric adjustment obligations).
- The Soviet Union refused to ratify partly because it "did not want to supply the data that was a requirement of membership" -- i.e., the Soviets understood the informational extraction mechanism.

**The screening mechanism**: Britain and other weak states made generous concessions (accepting dollar hegemony, smaller Fund size, asymmetric adjustment) because they were uncertain about the minimum terms the US would accept, and they desperately needed US participation. The consensus process under US informational advantage produced outcomes far more favorable to the US than formal power-weighted voting would suggest was "necessary."

### Key Sources
- Steil, B. (2013). *The Battle of Bretton Woods: John Maynard Keynes, Harry Dexter White, and the Making of a New World Order*. Princeton University Press.
- Federal Reserve History, "Creation of the Bretton Woods System."
- Bordo, M.D. and B. Eichengreen (1993). *A Retrospective on the Bretton Woods System*. University of Chicago Press.

### Evidence Strength: STRONG
Steil's archival account documents information manipulation, pre-conference intelligence extraction, and the gap between what the US needed vs. what it obtained. The consensus process is well-documented. The weakness: the Bretton Woods case is more about bilateral US-UK negotiation than true multilateral consensus with many weak states. Also, the US advantage was partly informational but largely about raw material power (gold reserves).

## CASE 3: OPEC Quota Negotiations -- Saudi Arabia as Swing Producer (MODERATE-STRONG)

### Organization & Episode
OPEC production quota allocations, especially 1982-1986 and ongoing OPEC+ negotiations. Decisions require consensus among member states.

### Players
- **H**: Saudi Arabia (world's largest oil exporter, dominant spare capacity)
- **W**: Smaller OPEC members (Nigeria, Angola, Ecuador, Iraq, Iran, etc.)

### theta (private information)
Saudi Arabia's true production capacity, extraction costs, and strategic reservation price for oil. The actual spare capacity figures are contested -- recent analysis suggests "actual deployable spare capacity is far lower than official figures suggest" and "the three agencies disagree by more than one million barrels in aggregate." Saudi Arabia's true cost of fiscal breakeven, its willingness to absorb production cuts, and its strategic horizon are all private information.

### alpha (outside option)
Saudi Arabia can flood markets unilaterally (as it did in 1985-86 under Yamani, crashing oil prices from $30 to $10), or restrict output to support prices. Either way, smaller producers cannot replicate this capacity. Saudi Arabia carries ~3 million bpd of spare capacity, "the largest single-country buffer in the world by a wide margin."

### Evidence of screening / overpayment / conservative offers

- **The 1982-86 swing producer episode**: Saudi Arabia agreed to be the "swing producer," cutting its own output from 10 million to 2 million bpd while other members cheated on quotas. This appears to be the OPPOSITE of screening rent -- Saudi Arabia bore the cost. BUT: the deeper story is that Saudi Arabia used its private information about long-run extraction costs and reserves to set a price policy that preserved long-term market share. Other members, not knowing Saudi Arabia's true strategic calculation, free-rode in the short term but ultimately faced Saudi retaliation.
- **Quota allocation disputes**: "The fuzzy math in calculating new targets stems from the outdated production capacity estimates" -- i.e., true production capacity is private information, and quota negotiations involve screening about each member's true capability.
- **Angola's 2023 exit**: Angola left OPEC over quota disagreements, suggesting that smaller members sometimes find the screening rent extracted by Saudi Arabia too costly.
- **2027 mechanism reform**: OPEC+ approved "a new mechanism to reassess the maximum sustainable production capacities of all its producers" -- this is literally an attempt to reduce information asymmetry in quota negotiations.

### Key Sources
- Yergin, D. (2008). *The Prize: The Epic Quest for Oil, Money, and Power*. Free Press (updated ed.).
- Adelman, M.A. (1995). *The Genie out of the Bottle: World Oil since 1970*. MIT Press.
- Fattouh, B. and A. Sen (2015). "Saudi Arabia and the Shifting Geoeconomics of Oil." Carnegie Endowment.
- EIA reports on OPEC spare capacity.

### Evidence Strength: MODERATE-STRONG
The informational asymmetry about true production capacity is well-documented and directly relevant. The consensus decision-making rule is institutional fact. However, the mapping to the paper's model is imperfect because: (a) Saudi Arabia sometimes acts as the accommodating party (swing producer) rather than the one extracting screening rent; (b) the private information is about production capacity rather than valuation of the agreement per se; (c) smaller producers sometimes cheat rather than making "conservative offers." The 2027 capacity reassessment reform is strong evidence that information asymmetry is recognized as a problem in quota negotiations.

## CASE 4: Basel I Accord (1988) -- US/UK vs. Japanese Banks (MODERATE)

### Organization & Episode
The 1988 Basel Capital Accord, negotiated within the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision. Decisions required consensus among G-10 central bank regulators.

### Players
- **H**: United States and United Kingdom (initiators who proposed the accord)
- **W**: Japan (whose banks were the primary targets), other G-10 members

### theta (private information)
The true capital adequacy position of Japanese banks, including "hidden reserves" (unrealized gains on equity holdings) and the real risk exposure of Japanese bank portfolios. Japanese accounting conventions allowed banks to count 70% of unrealized stock market gains as Tier 2 capital, making their true financial position opaque to outsiders.

### alpha (outside option)
US/UK could impose bilateral capital requirements on foreign banks operating in their markets, effectively excluding undercapitalized Japanese banks from the world's two largest financial centers.

### Evidence of screening / overpayment / conservative offers

**Oatley and Nabors (1998, IO)** argue:
- "Domestic politics rather than international market failure motivated American policymakers to propose international regulation."
- "Through international regulation the United States sought to redistribute income from Japanese to American commercial banks."
- Japanese banks' dependence on hidden reserves (unrealized gains on equity) made them vulnerable to externally imposed capital requirements. When the Nikkei fell in 1990-92, these "hidden reserves" evaporated.
- "Some observers saw [Basel I] as a conspiracy of certain Western banks in order to halt the international expansion of the relatively undercapitalized Japanese banks."

**The screening mechanism**: Japan's true bank capital was private information (hidden reserves, opaque accounting). The US/UK proposed 8% capital adequacy ratios knowing this would disproportionately constrain Japanese banks. Japan had to accept the consensus because the alternative (exclusion from US/UK financial markets) was worse. Japan effectively "overpaid" by accepting capital standards calibrated to US/UK banking structures.

### Key Sources
- Oatley, T. and R. Nabors (1998). "Redistributive Cooperation: Market Failure, Wealth Transfers, and the Basle Accord." *International Organization* 52(1): 35-54.
- Kapstein, E.B. (1989). "Resolving the Regulator's Dilemma." *International Organization* 43(2): 323-347.
- Singer, D.A. (2007). *Regulating Capital: Setting Standards for the International Financial System*. Cornell University Press.

### Evidence Strength: MODERATE
The informational asymmetry (hidden reserves) is well-documented. The consensus requirement and Japan's forced compliance are clear. However, the mapping is imperfect: here the "hegemon" (US/UK) uses information about the WEAK state's private type, rather than the hegemon possessing private information that gives it screening rent. This is closer to adverse selection by the agenda-setter than screening by the included party. Still useful as a contrasting case.

## CASE 5: UN Law of the Sea / International Seabed Authority (MODERATE)

### Organization & Episode
UNCLOS III negotiations (1973-1982), specifically the deep seabed mining regime (Part XI). The US ultimately refused to sign, demonstrating the model's logic in the negative.

### Players
- **H**: United States (dominant deep-sea mining technology and capability)
- **W**: Group of 77 developing countries (advocating "common heritage of mankind" principle)

### theta (private information)
The US's true valuation of deep seabed mining rights -- i.e., the commercial viability of manganese nodule extraction, the strategic importance of cobalt/nickel/copper for the military-industrial complex, and the expected return on mining investment. This was genuinely uncertain: deep-sea mining technology was nascent, and only US firms (and to a lesser extent, consortia from UK, Germany, Japan) had the technical knowledge to assess commercial viability.

### alpha (outside option)
The US could (and did) pursue unilateral deep-sea mining under domestic legislation (the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act of 1980), bypassing the multilateral regime entirely.

### Evidence of screening / overpayment / conservative offers

- The G-77 demanded technology transfer, profit sharing, and ISA control over mining -- in essence, demanding that the US reveal its private information (mining technology) and share its screening rent (profits).
- Reagan explicitly rejected this as developing countries "looking for a free ride at our expense."
- The 1994 Implementation Agreement significantly weakened Part XI provisions -- eliminating mandatory technology transfer and the Enterprise's special privileges -- as concessions to get US participation. These concessions were "conservative offers" by the G-77, accepting less favorable terms to attract the US.
- The US STILL has not ratified UNCLOS, partly because it lacks "de facto veto power" within the ISA.
- **Negative case**: The US chose the OUTSIDE OPTION (unilateral mining), precisely because the unanimity-like regime demanded too much information revelation. This illustrates the model's prediction: when alpha is high enough, H prefers exclusion.

### Key Sources
- Schmidt, M.G. (1989). *Common Heritage or Common Burden?* Oxford University Press.
- Sebenius, J.K. (1984). *Negotiating the Law of the Sea*. Harvard University Press.
- Steil, B. and M. Hinds (2009). *Money, Markets, and Sovereignty*. Yale University Press.
- Heritage Foundation (2012). "The U.S. Can Mine the Deep Seabed Without Joining UNCLOS."

### Evidence Strength: MODERATE
The informational asymmetry (mining technology and commercial viability) is genuine and documented. The consensus requirement was central. However, this is a NEGATIVE case -- the US refused to participate, which illustrates the model's outside option prediction but not the screening mechanism itself. The 1994 concessions by G-77 DO illustrate screening (conservative offers to attract H), but the US's continued non-ratification weakens the case.

## CASE 6: NATO Burden-Sharing and Article 5 Consensus (MODERATE-WEAK)

### Organization & Episode
NATO burden-sharing negotiations, especially since 2014 (2% GDP target) and accelerating under Trump administrations (2017-2021, 2025-present). All NATO decisions require consensus.

### Players
- **H**: United States (dominant military power, provider of nuclear umbrella and conventional deterrence)
- **W**: European allies, especially smaller NATO members

### theta (private information)
The US's true commitment to Article 5 collective defense. European allies face genuine uncertainty about whether the US would actually defend them in a crisis, and what the US truly values in the alliance (burden-sharing? geopolitical positioning? intelligence cooperation?). As analysts note: "the ambiguity of the American commitment may accumulate to the point that the guarantee of mutual support implicit in Article 5 loses its deterrent weight."

### alpha (outside option)
The US has repeatedly signaled (especially under Trump) that it could withdraw from NATO or reduce commitment. The US views its "strategic priorities in the Indo-Pacific" as competing with European defense commitments.

### Evidence of screening / overpayment / conservative offers

- NATO allies agreed to raise defense spending targets from 2% to 3.5% (core) + 1.5% (security) = 5% of GDP by 2035 -- a dramatic increase that exceeds what pure security logic would require.
- European allies consistently make concessions on defense spending precisely because they are UNCERTAIN about the US commitment level. If they knew the US would stay regardless, they would free-ride more aggressively.
- The consensus requirement means the US cannot be "outvoted" -- every ally must agree, giving the US veto power that amplifies its informational advantage.
- Smaller allies worry that the US "may seek a peace-settlement in Ukraine that requires extraordinary European effort to police."

### Key Sources
- Sandler, T. and K. Hartley (1999). *The Political Economy of NATO*. Cambridge University Press.
- NATO official communiques on burden-sharing.
- FOI Memo 8507, "NATO decision-making: Is the consensus rule still fit for purpose?"

### Evidence Strength: MODERATE-WEAK
The uncertainty about US commitment is real and well-documented. The consensus rule is institutional fact. However, the mechanism here is more about CREDIBLE THREATS than private information about cooperation value. The US's reservation value for NATO participation is not really "private" in the model's sense -- it's more about strategic ambiguity regarding commitment. Also, the directionality is somewhat reversed: the W states are the ones making offers to the H (higher spending) to keep H in the alliance, which does map to the model, but the "private information" element is weaker.

## CASE 7: Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968) (WEAK-MODERATE)

### Organization & Episode
Eighteen Nation Committee on Disarmament negotiations (1965-1968) leading to the NPT. Required broad consensus including both US/USSR (nuclear states) and non-nuclear states.

### Players
- **H**: US and USSR jointly (nuclear weapon states, NWS)
- **W**: Non-nuclear weapon states (NNWS), especially India, Brazil, developing nations

### theta (private information)
The NWS's true intentions regarding disarmament (Article VI), technology transfer (Article IV), and the extent of civilian nuclear assistance they would actually provide. The NWS had private information about the feasibility and value of "peaceful nuclear explosions" and civilian nuclear technology.

### alpha (outside option)
NWS could maintain nuclear monopoly regardless of treaty. NNWS who refused (India, Israel, Pakistan) pursued independent nuclear programs.

### Evidence of screening / overpayment / conservative offers

- "Nuclear states hesitated in sharing nuclear technology with developing nations, even technology that could be used for peaceful applications."
- NNWS accepted a fundamentally asymmetric treaty (renouncing weapons in exchange for technology transfer and disarmament promises) under uncertainty about whether NWS would fulfill their commitments.
- A 1968 non-nuclear weapon state conference was held in "an atmosphere of bitterness, disappointment and resentment."
- "After two years of negotiations, the nuclear powers managed to make enough concessions to induce many non-nuclear powers to sign."
- Holdouts (India, Brazil, Argentina, Israel) were precisely those states whose private valuation of nuclear weapons was highest relative to the technology transfer on offer.

### Key Sources
- Goldschmidt, B. (1980). "The Negotiation of the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)." IAEA Bulletin.
- NSA Electronic Briefing Book No. 253, "40th Anniversary of the Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty."
- State Department history: "The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), 1968."

### Evidence Strength: WEAK-MODERATE
The asymmetric information about technology transfer commitments is documented. However, the NPT case does not clearly map to the model: (a) the "consensus" was more about universal accession than a formal voting rule; (b) the screening mechanism is about the NNWS being uncertain about NWS commitment, not about the hegemon extracting screening rent from inclusion; (c) the NWS's informational advantage is about capabilities rather than valuation. Better suited as context/contrast than as a primary illustration.

## CASE 8: Dassler, Heinkelmann-Wild & Huysmans (2025) -- Theoretical Framework (SECONDARY)

### Not a case but a theoretical paper

**"Insuring the Weak: The Institutional Power Equilibrium in International Organizations"** published in *International Studies Quarterly* 69(1), 2025.

### Relevance to the paper's mechanism

This paper develops a theory that directly addresses why weak states accept institutional arrangements dominated by powerful states. The key insight: weak states obtain "formal safeguards against exploitation by the powerful through the inclusion of veto or exit rights." The paper specifies an "institutional power equilibrium" at the heart of IOs' constitutional treaties.

This is the closest existing theoretical work to the paper's mechanism from the IR side (as opposed to the game theory side). The key difference: Dassler et al. focus on veto/exit as INSURANCE for weak states, while the paper's model shows that unanimity/consensus serves as a SCREENING DEVICE that benefits the hegemon. These are complementary perspectives -- weak states accept unanimity thinking it protects them (Dassler et al.), while the hegemon accepts it because it generates screening rent (the paper's contribution).

### Key Source
- Dassler, B., T. Heinkelmann-Wild, and M. Huysmans (2025). "Insuring the Weak: The Institutional Power Equilibrium in International Organizations." *International Studies Quarterly* 69(1).

## RECOMMENDATIONS FOR THE PAPER

### For the Discussion section (GATT/WTO frase revision, RIO-4b):
The GATT/WTO case is the strongest. Steinberg's explicit statement that "decision making rules have been maintained because they help generate information used by powerful states in the agenda-setting process" is remarkably close to the paper's mechanism. The paper's Discussion can cite this as evidence that consensus operates as an information-generating technology benefiting the powerful, consistent with the model's prediction.

### For broader empirical motivation:
- **Bretton Woods** is the best single case study because the informational manipulation (White's draft substitutions, pre-conference intelligence extraction, Keynes's inability to read the final text) is dramatically documented.
- **OPEC** is useful because the private information (spare production capacity) maps cleanly to theta.
- **Basel I** is useful as a contrasting case where the "screening" goes in the other direction.

### Honest limitations:
In none of these cases is the EXACT mechanism (W makes conservative offer due to uncertainty about H's reservation value under unanimity) documented in pure form with the explicit language of the model. The historical evidence generally shows:
- Power asymmetry operating through consensus (Steinberg)
- Information manipulation by the powerful (Steil)
- Uncertainty about true capacity/commitment (OPEC, NATO)

The paper's distinctive contribution is to formalize HOW informational asymmetry translates into screening rent specifically through the unanimity requirement. The historical cases provide motivation rather than definitive proof.
