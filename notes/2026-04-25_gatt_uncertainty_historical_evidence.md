# Historical Evidence: Uncertainty at the GATT's Creation (1946-1948)

## Research Question

At the GATT's creation, did negotiators already know that trade liberalization was valuable (high prior), or was there genuine uncertainty about whether multilateral cooperation would produce gains?

**Answer**: The evidence reveals a complex picture. There was HIGH confidence that trade liberalization *in principle* was valuable (the "lesson of the 1930s"). But there was DEEP uncertainty about whether *this particular multilateral framework* would work -- whether it could be negotiated, whether it would be ratified, whether partner countries would comply, and whether the institutional design was correct. This distinction maps well onto the paper's framework: the prior about the *value of cooperation* (V) was arguably moderate-to-uncertain, because even true believers acknowledged the framework was untested, fragile, and might fail.

---

## PART I: EVIDENCE FOR HIGH PRIOR -- "We Know Trade Is Good"

### 1. Cordell Hull's Lifelong Conviction (The Ideological Foundation)

Hull served as Secretary of State from 1933 to 1944 and was the intellectual architect of postwar trade liberalization. His 1948 memoirs contain the foundational statement:

> "When the war came in 1914, I was very soon impressed with two points... I saw that you could not separate the idea of commerce from the idea of war and peace... and that wars were often largely caused by economic rivalry conducted unfairly... **From then on, to me, unhampered trade dovetailed with peace; high tariffs, trade barriers, and unfair economic competition, with war.**"

**Source**: Cordell Hull, *The Memoirs of Cordell Hull* (New York: Macmillan, 1948), pp. 81, 84. Cited in Douglas A. Irwin, "Trade Liberalization: Cordell Hull and the Case for Optimism," CFR Working Paper No. 4 (July 2008), p. 3.

Hull's conviction was not idiosyncratic -- it became the intellectual foundation for US trade policy. His protege Will Clayton and the entire State Department trade apparatus inherited this worldview.

### 2. The "Lesson of the 1930s" Consensus

By 1945, there was near-consensus among Anglo-American policymakers that the Smoot-Hawley tariff (1930) and the subsequent trade wars had deepened the Great Depression and contributed to the rise of fascism. This "lesson" was treated as established fact.

Hull himself "felt that the Smoot-Hawley tariff legislation of 1930 had been a cause of the Great Depression" and "when he got to the State Department, he realized that Smoot-Hawley had produced indignation throughout the world and had caused many countries to retaliate by raising their own tariffs."

**Source**: Irwin, "Trade Liberalization: Cordell Hull and the Case for Optimism," CFR Working Paper No. 4 (2008); see also Douglas A. Irwin, *Peddling Protectionism: Smoot-Hawley and the Great Depression* (Princeton UP, 2011).

### 3. The 1945 State Department "Proposals for Expansion of World Trade"

On December 6, 1945, the US State Department published its "Proposals for Expansion of World Trade and Employment," which stated:

> "Unless long-term trade and employment policies are agreed upon soon, there is **grave danger** that the pressing economic problems of the transition period, already upon us, may be solved by resort to **measures of expediency** which will tend to become permanent and thus **seriously hamper later efforts at constructive international cooperation** in these fields."

**Source**: U.S. Department of State, "Proposals for Expansion of World Trade and Employment" (December 6, 1945). Available at FRASER (Federal Reserve Archival System for Economic Research): fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/historical/eccles/036_04_0003.pdf. Also in FRUS, 1945, Vol. II, Doc. 670.

This document reveals HIGH confidence that trade liberalization was desirable, but simultaneously reveals anxiety that the window for achieving it was narrow.

### 4. Irwin, Mavroidis & Sykes on the Framers' Beliefs

The most systematic academic reconstruction of the framers' intentions concludes:

> "The framers sought trade liberalization and negotiated reductions in trade barriers as a way to **expand international trade to promote world prosperity**, restrict the use of discriminatory policies to reduce conflict over trade, and thereby **establish economic foundations for maintaining world peace**."

> "The framers understood the GATT as a **pro-peace instrument**; however, they were mindful of the costs of achieving such a far-reaching objective and were not willing to allocate them disproportionately."

**Source**: Douglas A. Irwin, Petros C. Mavroidis, and Alan O. Sykes, *The Genesis of the GATT* (Cambridge UP, 2008), Introduction and Conclusion.

## PART II: EVIDENCE FOR LOW/UNCERTAIN PRIOR -- "We Don't Know If This Will Work"

### 5. The GATT as a Provisional "Stopgap" -- Never Meant to Be Permanent

The GATT was explicitly designed as a temporary measure. It was applied under a "Protocol of Provisional Application" while the more ambitious ITO Charter was being negotiated. The GATT, as an international treaty, **never formally entered into force**.

> "The Protocol had been conceived of as providing a **temporary dispensation** to enable contracting parties to apply Part II of the General Agreement without changing existing legislation."

The architects themselves did not expect the GATT to survive as the permanent framework. It was, in WTO historian Roy Santana's words, a **"stopgap measure"** that was supposed to be absorbed by the ITO. The fact that it was designed as provisional reveals that even its creators were uncertain about whether it would endure.

**Source**: WTO, "Provisional Application of the General Agreement" (analytical note); Roy Santana, "70th Anniversary of the GATT: Stalin, the Marshall Plan, and the Provisional Application of the GATT 1947," WTO (2017). See: wto.org/english/tratop_e/gatt_e/stalin_marshall_conclude_negotiations_e.htm

### 6. The GATT Negotiations "Appeared Doomed to Fail" (September 1947)

The negotiations nearly collapsed over the US-UK clash on imperial preferences. WTO historical research documents:

> "Convinced of the fairness of their positions, the Americans and the British dug in their heels and grew deeply entrenched. **Clayton had had enough with British stubbornness and proposed to walk away from the negotiating table. Short of a miracle, the GATT negotiations appeared doomed to fail.**"

Sir Stafford Cripps (UK Board of Trade President) told Clayton that the UK Cabinet contained "a **large number of people who would not shed tears if the negotiation broke down altogether**."

The resolution came only because Secretary of State George Marshall personally intervened, instructing Clayton not to insist on immediate elimination of imperial preferences.

**Source**: Roy Santana, "Clash of the GATT Negotiators" and "How Stalin and the Marshall Plan Helped to Conclude the Negotiations," WTO historical articles (2017). See: wto.org/english/tratop_e/gatt_e/clash_gatt_negotiators_e.htm

### 7. British Cabinet Skepticism and the Imperial Preference Dilemma

The British government's internal uncertainty was profound. Richard Toye's archival research shows:

- British policymakers faced a fundamental dilemma: "**to what degree should Britain accept restrictions on her own freedoms as the price of achieving a more satisfactory international economic environment**."
- Cripps "strongly opposed the dogmatism of the 'American free traders'" and feared that "an immediate elimination of tariff preferences would lead to a **ruinous trade deficit and a balance of payments crisis**."
- The UK's economic situation was dire: the 1947 "dollar crisis" had nearly bankrupted Britain, making concessions on imperial preference politically toxic.

**Source**: Richard Toye, "The Attlee Government, the Imperial Preference System and the Creation of the GATT," *English Historical Review* 118, no. 478 (September 2003): 912-939.

### 8. Will Clayton's Own Admission: "We Grossly Underestimated"

Even the lead US negotiator acknowledged fundamental uncertainty about the economic conditions underlying the trade framework. In his famous May 27, 1947 memorandum to Under Secretary Acheson:

> "It is now obvious that **we grossly underestimated the destruction to the European economy by the war**. We understood the physical destruction, but we **failed to take fully into account** the effects of economic dislocation on production -- nationalization of industries, drastic land reform, severance of long-standing commercial ties, disappearance of private commercial firms through death or loss of capital, etc."

> "**Without further prompt and substantial aid from the United States, economic, social and political disintegration will overwhelm Europe.**"

This memo, written *during* the Geneva negotiations, reveals that the very economic foundations on which the trade framework was being built were understood to be far more fragile than expected.

**Source**: William L. Clayton, Memorandum to Under Secretary of State Dean Acheson, "The European Crisis," May 27, 1947. FRUS, 1947, Vol. III, Doc. 136. Available at: history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v03/d136

### 9. The Failure of the ITO -- Ultimate Evidence of Uncertainty

The International Trade Organization (ITO), which was supposed to be the permanent institutional framework for trade, **failed completely**. The Havana Charter was signed in March 1948 by 56 countries, but:

- On December 6, 1950, President Truman announced he would no longer seek Congressional approval.
- No other state ratified after the US withdrawal.
- The ITO was killed by an unusual coalition: "**both staunch protectionists and those advocates of international trade liberalization, who did not welcome the version of the Charter adopted in Havana**."

The opposition included:
- **Protectionists**: who feared trade liberalization would harm domestic industry
- **Free-trade "perfectionists"**: who felt the Charter made too many concessions to state intervention
- **Philip Cortney** (French-American economist): published *The Economic Munich: The ITO Charter, Inflation or Liberty, the 1929 Lesson* (1949), comparing ITO ratification to appeasement. Business economists who shared his view "castigated Havana Charter supporters as Keynesian zealots, ignorants or 'do-gooders'."

**Sources**: 
- Wikipedia, "International Trade Organization" (with extensive primary source citations)
- Philip Cortney, *The Economic Munich: The ITO Charter, Inflation or Liberty, the 1929 Lesson* (New York: Philosophical Library, 1949)
- William Diebold, "The End of the ITO," *Essays in International Finance* No. 16 (Princeton: International Finance Section, October 1952)
- WTO, "The GATT Years: From Havana to Marrakesh": wto.org/english/thewto_e/whatis_e/tif_e/fact4_e.htm

### 10. Developing Country Skepticism at Havana (1947-1948)

At the Havana Conference, developing countries pushed for extensive exceptions to trade liberalization:

- Latin American countries "believed that their fragile economies should face the strong economies of the industrialized countries by economic planning and import substitution, already in place in several Latin American countries since the 1930s and the 1940s."
- India and Pakistan received special interim trade provisions as newly independent states.
- A British official noted that "the US had given way on things which were of advantage to India and Latin American countries in allowing them to build up their industries under protection."
- The final Charter reflected "the growing political power" of underdeveloped countries, and "in choosing to look for some degree of compromise with the latter, the interests of the former had to some degree to be sacrificed."

This skepticism was not about whether trade *per se* was good, but about whether *multilateral trade liberalization would benefit countries at different development levels* -- a direct form of uncertainty about the value of this particular cooperation.

**Source**: Richard Toye, "Developing Multilateralism: The Havana Charter and the Fight for the International Trade Organization, 1947-1948," *International History Review* 25, no. 2 (2003): 282-305.

### 11. Congressional Skepticism and the Republican Opposition

The Republican Party took control of Congress in the 1946 midterm elections, creating a hostile environment:

- "A trade treaty would have to garner two-thirds Senate support -- **a formidable task given implacable Republican hostility to the proposed ITO**."
- Clayton himself "was concerned that such a deal would not be approved by Congress."
- Resistance to the tariff cuts negotiated at Geneva made it "**a most inopportune time to present such a treaty to Congress**."
- Wilcox reported to the US delegation that amendments had been made to the Charter to "meet suggestions from the Senate Finance Committee, Foreign Trade Council, and other interested parties" -- evidence of ongoing accommodation to skeptics.

**Source**: FRUS, 1947, Vol. I, Docs. 463, 486; Roy Santana, WTO historical articles (2017).

### 12. Clair Wilcox: "The Most Complicated Set of International Negotiations in History"

At a meeting of the US delegation in Geneva, Wilcox himself characterized the enterprise:

> "We have undertaken **the most complicated set of international negotiations in the history of mankind**. There are no less than 100 sets of negotiations among the 18 countries participating in the Geneva Conference proceeding at the same time."

This characterization reveals the unprecedented and untested nature of the enterprise.

**Source**: FRUS, 1947, Vol. I, Doc. 486, Minutes of a Meeting of the United States Delegation, Geneva. Available at: history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v01/d486

## PART III: THE "EMBEDDED LIBERALISM" COMPROMISE -- Evidence of Structural Uncertainty

### 13. Ruggie's "Embedded Liberalism" Framework

John Ruggie's influential analysis identifies the postwar trade order as a compromise born of fundamental uncertainty:

> "The task of postwar institutional reconstruction was to **maneuver between economic nationalism and classical liberalism**, devising a framework that would safeguard domestic stability without triggering the mutually destructive external consequences of the interwar period."

> "Unlike the economic nationalism of the 1930s, it would be multilateral in character; unlike the liberalism of the gold standard and free trade, its multilateralism would be **predicated upon domestic interventionism**."

The very need for this compromise -- embedding liberalism within a framework of domestic protections (escape clauses, balance-of-payments exceptions, Article XXI security exceptions) -- reveals that the framers were NOT confident that pure trade liberalization would work. They built in extensive safety valves precisely because they were uncertain.

**Source**: John Gerard Ruggie, "International Regimes, Transactions, and Change: Embedded Liberalism in the Postwar Economic Order," *International Organization* 36, no. 2 (Spring 1982): 379-415.

## PART IV: SYNTHESIS -- What Kind of Uncertainty?

The evidence suggests a layered structure of beliefs:

| Dimension | Prior Level | Evidence |
|-----------|------------|---------|
| Trade liberalization is *theoretically* good | HIGH | Hull's conviction, "lesson of the 1930s," State Dept. consensus |
| *This particular framework* will succeed in being negotiated | LOW-MODERATE | Near-collapse over preferences, "doomed to fail," Cripps' indifference |
| Partner countries will *comply* and *reciprocate* | UNCERTAIN | UK intransigence, developing country demands for exceptions |
| *Congress/domestic publics* will ratify | LOW | Republican opposition, ITO failure, Clayton's own fears |
| *Benefits will materialize* for all participants | UNCERTAIN | European economic crisis, "grossly underestimated" destruction, dollar gap |
| The *institutional design* is correct | UNCERTAIN | Provisional application, ITO vs. GATT debate, "perfectionists" vs. "protectionists" |

### Implication for the Paper

For the paper's Bayesian Persuasion framework, the GATT case fits well as a scenario where:

1. **H (the hegemon/US) had a moderately high prior** about V(theta) -- they believed trade cooperation was valuable but were uncertain about the magnitude of gains from *this specific* framework.

2. **W (weak states/other negotiators) had genuine uncertainty** -- the UK Cabinet wouldn't "shed tears" if it failed; developing countries wanted extensive exceptions; the framework was designed as provisional precisely because no one was sure it would work.

3. **The ITO failure is the counterfactual** -- the more ambitious version of the framework was rejected, suggesting that even among those who believed in trade, there was profound uncertainty about *which institutional form* would deliver value.

4. **Bayesian Persuasion was arguably at work**: the US (H) needed to persuade skeptical partners that cooperation would be valuable, using its private information about the economic landscape (Clayton's intimate knowledge of European economic conditions, US capacity to provide Marshall Plan aid as a complement to trade liberalization).
