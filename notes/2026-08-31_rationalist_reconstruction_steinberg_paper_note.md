# A Rationalist Reconstruction of Informal Power in the WTO

**Date:** 2026-08-31  
**Status:** Paper-ready conceptual note; not yet integrated into the manuscript.  
**Purpose:** Translate the practices catalogued by Steinberg (2002) into a
small set of strategic primitives, in the spirit of Fearon's (1995)
rationalist reconstruction of explanations for war. The exercise distinguishes
empirical manifestations of power from genuinely distinct mechanisms and
clarifies the contribution of the baseline model and its agenda-power
extension.

## 1. The analytical move

@steinberg2002shadow documents many practices through which formally equal WTO members
nevertheless exercise unequal influence. A list of practices, however, is not
yet a list of distinct strategic explanations. Different practices may
instantiate the same underlying primitive, while a single historical episode
may combine several primitives. Following the analytical strategy in
@fearon1995rationalist, the relevant question is therefore:

> Under rational behavior, what element of the bargaining game must each
> practice change in order to affect the agreement or its distribution?

Consider an abstract bargaining environment

\[
\Gamma=\langle X,d,\delta,\rho,q,\mathcal I,E\rangle,
\]

where $X$ is the contract space, $d$ is the vector of disagreement or
continuation payoffs, $\delta$ captures patience, $\rho$ is the recognition,
proposal, and amendment protocol, $q$ is the approval threshold,
$\mathcal I$ is the information structure, and $E$ summarizes enforcement,
implementation, and future interaction. An informal practice constitutes an
independent rationalist mechanism only if it changes one of these objects or
supplies a strategic reason for selecting among otherwise equivalent outcomes.

This reconstruction should also distinguish two questions that are often
conflated:

1. Why can powerful states obtain favorable outcomes *within* a consensus
   institution?
2. Why would a powerful state prefer consensus to majority rule?

A practice may answer the first question without answering the second. Agenda
control, for example, can generate distributive power under either voting rule.
It explains a preference for consensus only if its value differs systematically
across approval rules.

## 3. Three corrections that should govern the paper's presentation

### 3.1 Acquiescence and estoppel are not separate mechanisms

Steinberg describes a practice in which a state that fails to object, reserve
its position, or request an amendment may later face an argument that it
acquiesced. A rationalist reconstruction should not treat that legal label as a
source of commitment on its own. There are only two coherent possibilities.

First, the proposal has reached the formal decision stage and the rule defines
the absence of objection as approval. The decision is then final because the
institutional rule says so. Second, negotiations remain open. In that case, a
rational state can revise its position unless another explicit rule limits
reconsideration. If powerful states want non-objection at time (t) to eliminate
the right to object at time (t+1), that restriction must enter the game as a
deadline or irreversible procedural rule. The mechanism is the rule, not
estoppel.

The informational interpretation survives this critique. A proposal can serve
as a probe when it reaches a stage at which states must choose among approval,
objection, and amendment. Their observable choices may reveal information.
This is simply voting and amendment behavior in a game of incomplete
information; acquiescence has no additional strategic content.

### 3.2 Reputation belongs to a repeated game

In a one-shot bargaining game, rejecting an offer below one's continuation
value is not opportunistic or socially embarrassing. It is the rational exercise
of a participation constraint. If agreement is sufficiently valuable, the
proposer should compensate the necessary voter. A reputational mechanism is
rational only when present behavior changes future payoffs, for example through
future punishment, exclusion, reduced trust, or belief updating about a durable
type. In reduced form, the current cost of blocking would have to equal the
discounted loss of future cooperation, not an unexplained dislike of appearing
obstructionist. Since the paper does not model repeated interaction, this channel
should remain outside the baseline and agenda extension.

### 3.3 Single undertaking and destruction of the fallback are distinct

The single undertaking is a general rule for packaging and closing a trade
round. The Doha Round was launched under the same principle even though there
was no analogous withdrawal from GATT 1947. The rule therefore cannot be
identified with destruction of the status quo.

Analytically, single undertaking has two related effects. During negotiation,
cross-issue linkage permits compensation across dimensions and makes the
aggregate-surplus representation more plausible. At closure, however, members
face an indivisible choice over the completed package rather than an à-la-carte
menu. Thus the contents of the package can be divisible even though the final
acceptance decision is not.

The closure of the Uruguay Round combined this general packaging rule with a
separate exercise of power. By withdrawing from GATT 1947 after creating the
WTO, the United States and the European Community reduced the value of the old
fallback for countries that refused the new package. The first operation concerns
the contract space and closure rule; the second concerns disagreement payoffs.

## 4. What the reconstruction implies for the contribution

Seen through this lens, Steinberg does not offer more than twenty independent
explanations of WTO power. He documents many empirical manifestations of a
smaller number of strategic families:

1. agenda and amendment power;
2. outside-option power and patience;
3. transferability and the contract space;
4. information acquisition and screening;
5. commitment and repeated interaction; and
6. participation, implementation, and compliance.

The baseline model accepts two elements that Steinberg helps microfound. Market
power motivates asymmetric disagreement values, and side payments plus issue
linkage motivate representation of the agreement as a divisible surplus. The
baseline then deliberately removes hegemonic agenda control. This is not a claim
that agenda power is absent from the WTO. It is a strategy for identifying what
formal equality can do when a familiar source of informal inequality is switched
off.

What remains is a distinct interaction between the information structure and the
approval rule. Majority permits a proposer to replace the hegemon with other
votes. Unanimity makes the hegemon's approval indispensable. When the hegemon
privately knows the value of its continuation, weak proposers must bargain over a
necessary input whose reservation value they do not observe. The institutional
source of informational power is therefore not secrecy alone, but secrecy about
a non-substitutable approval.

The agenda-power extension then reconnects the model to the most prominent
family of practices in Steinberg. It should preserve the distinction between the
direct value of proposal rights and the value of private information. For each
rule $g \in \{M,U\}$, define informational rent relative to the corresponding
complete-information game with the same agenda protocol:

\[
RI_g(\theta)
=
V_g^{\mathrm{private}}(\theta)
-
V_g^{\mathrm{public}}(\theta).
\]

The institutional comparison can then be written as

\[
V_U^{\mathrm{private}}(\theta)-V_M^{\mathrm{private}}(\theta)
=
\left[V_U^{\mathrm{public}}(\theta)-V_M^{\mathrm{public}}(\theta)\right]
+
\left[RI_U(\theta)-RI_M(\theta)\right].
\]

The first bracket captures the direct institutional and agenda component under
public information. The second captures the differential informational rent.
Agenda power may change both terms and may interact non-additively with the
voting rule, but the entire payoff advantage should not be labeled informational.

## 5. Proposed paper-ready positioning

> Steinberg documents numerous practices through which formally equal WTO
> members exercise unequal influence. A rationalist reconstruction shows that
> most of these practices instantiate a smaller set of strategic primitives:
> proposal and amendment rights, asymmetric disagreement payoffs, a rich space
> of compensating concessions, information acquisition, and future enforcement.
> We take the asymmetry of disagreement values and the divisibility of the
> negotiated surplus as features of the bargaining environment, while initially
> removing formal proposal power from the hegemon. This permits us to isolate a
> different source of distributive advantage. Majority rule supplies substitute
> votes; unanimity does not. Uncertainty about the reservation value of an
> indispensable approval can therefore generate informational rent even when the
> informed hegemon does not control the agenda. We then restore hegemonic agenda
> power and examine how proposal rights alter both the complete-information
> benchmark and the informational rent generated by the voting rule.

## 6. Recommended use in the manuscript

- Use a shortened version of the table in the main text, organized by strategic
  families rather than by all twenty-two empirical items.
- Preserve the complete item-by-item crosswalk in an appendix or online
  supplement.
- Include a column asking whether each mechanism explains unequal influence
  under consensus or a preference for consensus over majority.
- Treat acquiescence and estoppel as part of the voting and amendment stage, not
  as an independent commitment mechanism.
- Treat reputational costs as a repeated-game extension.
- Keep single undertaking separate from the Uruguay Round withdrawal from GATT
  1947.
- Present Steinberg as supplying empirical microfoundations and rival mechanisms,
  not as offering a single formal alternative that the model merely replaces.
