Literature Review: BP + Legislative Bargaining + IO Design

Literature Review: Bayesian Persuasion, Legislative Bargaining, and Institutional Design in IOs

Date: 2026-04-21

1. Overview

This paper asks: why would a hegemon choose consensus (unanimity) in an international organization? The mechanism combines three literatures: (1) Bayesian Persuasion (information design), (2) Baron-Ferejohn legislative bargaining (coalition formation, exclusion), and (3) institutional design of IOs (voting rules, power asymmetry). Under unanimity, W must include H in the coalition, creating screening (aggressive vs. conservative offer) that generates non-convexity in H’s payoff. BP exploits this non-convexity. Under majority, W excludes H, eliminating screening and making BP ineffective. H prefers unanimity not despite the constraints, but because of them: unanimity is a technology that activates informational power to substitute for agenda power.

No existing paper combines all four elements: (i) multilateral BF bargaining, (ii) endogenous voting rule choice, (iii) Bayesian Persuasion by an informed player, and (iv) screening endogenously created by the voting rule. The closest papers occupy adjacent spaces.

3. Central Debates

Debate 1: Why do IOs adopt unanimity/consensus?

Position A — Self-enforcement (Maggi & Morelli 2006): Without external enforcement, unanimity is the best self-enforcing rule when the discount factor is low. All members behind a veil of ignorance choose unanimity to prevent defection. Mechanism: compliance incentives, not information.

Position B — Power preservation (Steinberg 2002): Powerful states prefer consensus because it generates information useful for agenda-setting (“invisible weighting”). US negotiators explicitly rejected weighted voting at the WTO in 1990 because consensus provides better information about weak states’ preferences.

Position C — Opacity/democracy (Gould 2016): The post-1990 rise of consensus reflects demand for opacity by democratic governments, not power preservation by hegemons. Empirically, US membership is not a significant predictor of consensus adoption.

Position D — Conventional inefficiency view (Miettinen & Vanberg 2025): Unanimity is uniquely inefficient due to strategic precommitment causing delay. All other rules (including all-but-one) are fully efficient.

Our contribution: Unanimity is chosen by the hegemon because it activates informational power (screening + BP). This is complementary to Maggi & Morelli (enforcement) and Steinberg (information), but provides a formal mechanism. It directly counters the inefficiency view by showing that the “costs” of unanimity (screening, veto) are features, not bugs.

Debate 2: How does information create power in bargaining?

Position A — Procedural information (Ali, Bernheim & Fan 2019): Predictability of the recognition process concentrates power in the current proposer under majority. Key result: if at least q players can be ruled out as next proposer, the first proposer captures all surplus. Under unanimity, predictability is neutralized.

Position B — Substantive information (Kim, Kim & Van Weelden 2025): BP augments agenda-setting power in veto bargaining. Proposer designs information about the state to manipulate vetoer’s acceptance decision. Binary signal is optimal.

Position C — Expert advice (Schnakenberg 2015, 2017): Experts exploit voting rules through strategic information transmission. All voting rules allowing collective preference cycles allow welfare-reducing manipulation.

Our contribution: Information about the state of the world (theta) creates power through screening under unanimity. Sharp contrast with Ali et al.: procedural information empowers the proposer under majority; substantive private information empowers the informed party under unanimity. Complements Kim et al. by adding multilateral bargaining, coalition formation, and endogenous institutional choice.

Debate 3: BP under different voting rules

Position A — Voters prefer unanimity (Alonso & Camara 2016): Voters may prefer supermajority to discipline the persuader. Under non-unanimous rules, the politician exploits voter heterogeneity.

Position B — Unanimity enables information transmission (Wong, Yang & Zhao 2024; Agranov & Tergiman 2019): Unanimity uniquely enables informative equilibria in committee settings. Communication under unanimity eliminates inefficiencies.

Position C — Private signals exploit majority (Chan et al. 2019; Kerman et al. 2024): Under majority with heterogeneous voters, the persuader uses private correlated signals to create pivotal voters. With a veto player, public communication is optimal.

Our contribution: The voting rule determines whether screening occurs, which in turn determines whether BP has value. This is a structural result about the interaction of institutions and information, not just about optimal signal design given a fixed rule.

5. Gaps Identified

Gaps Filled by This Paper

  1. No model combining BP + BF bargaining + voting rule choice. Kim et al. (2025) have BP + bargaining but no voting rule comparison. Alonso & Camara (2016) have BP + voting rules but no surplus bargaining. Glynia et al. (2026) have unanimity vs majority under incomplete info but no BP. Our paper uniquely occupies the intersection.

  2. No formal mechanism for Steinberg’s (2002) information-generation argument. Steinberg documents that powerful states prefer consensus because it generates information, but provides no formal model. Our screening + BP mechanism formalizes this.

  3. The “conventional” view that unanimity is inefficient (Miettinen & Vanberg 2025) lacks information channel. When the hegemon has private information, unanimity activates screening that makes BP profitable, potentially reversing the inefficiency result.

  4. Eraslan & Evdokimov (2019) explicitly identify asymmetric information in legislative bargaining as a “challenging and important avenue for future research.” Our paper directly addresses this gap.

  5. No paper models the hegemon’s choice of voting rule as information-power optimization. Maggi & Morelli (2006) explain unanimity from symmetric members’ perspective (enforcement). Alonso & Camara (2016) explain unanimity from receivers’ perspective (disciplining sender). Our paper explains unanimity from the sender/hegemon’s perspective (activating informational power).

Remaining Gaps in the Literature

  1. Dynamics: Multi-round information design in repeated BF bargaining (connects to Vosooghi 2022 on dynamic climate agreements).
  2. Heterogeneous weak states: Different outside options, information, or preferences among W’s (connects to Ali et al. 2019 on heterogeneous continuation values).
  3. Endogenous information acquisition: W’s invest to learn theta, eroding H’s informational advantage (our “paper futuro” on Doha).
  4. Empirical testing: Cross-IO variation in voting rules, information asymmetry, and bargaining outcomes (connects to Blake & Payton 2015 dataset).

6. Key Papers for Positioning

Must-Cite (direct overlap or contrast)

  1. Kim, Kim & Van Weelden (2025) — Closest existing work. BP in veto bargaining. We add: multilateral BF, voting rule choice, screening, endogenous institutional design. They take veto as given; we explain why H chooses it.

  2. Maggi & Morelli (2006) — Main alternative theory. Unanimity from enforcement. We provide complementary mechanism: informational power. Their symmetric members vs. our asymmetric hegemon.

  3. Steinberg (2002) — Empirical motivation. Consensus generates information for powerful states. We formalize this.

  4. Ali, Bernheim & Fan (2019) — Sharp contrast. Procedural predictability empowers proposer under majority; unanimity neutralizes. We show: private information empowers informed party under unanimity; majority neutralizes. Mirror image.

  5. Alonso & Camara (2016) — BP + voting. Receivers prefer unanimity to discipline sender. We show: sender prefers unanimity to activate screening. Mirror image.

  6. Glynia, Thum & Xefteris (2026) — Unanimity vs majority under incomplete info. Complementary: their proposer uncertainty vs. our H’s private info + BP.

  7. Kalandrakis (2006) — Proposal power in BF. Exclusion under majority is the mechanism we rely on.
